You’ve probably seen graphs all around you—on social media, in sports stats, or even tracking the weather. But did you know those graphs are often showing us how a function behaves? In this lesson, you’ll learn how to read and understand **graphs and tables** to figure out what a function is doing—whether it’s increasing, decreasing, staying the same, or changing direction. You’ll also explore **intercepts**, which tell you when a function crosses the axes, and how to recognize patterns and **trends** in the data.

Think of this lesson as learning how to “read between the lines” of a function’s story.

---

## **Learning Goals**

By the end of this lesson, you’ll be able to:

* Analyze graphs to describe how a function behaves over time.
* Identify key features like **increasing** and **decreasing** intervals.
* Find the **x-intercepts** and **y-intercepts** of a function.
* Use tables to find patterns, and match them to what’s happening on the graph.
* Make sense of real-world situations using function graphs.

---

## **What Does the Graph of a Function Tell You?**

A graph is like a picture of a function’s behavior. It shows you how one value (the output, or y) changes based on another value (the input, or x). By looking at the shape and direction of the graph, you can figure out a lot:

* Is it going **up**? That means the function is **increasing**.
* Is it going **down**? Then it’s **decreasing**.
* Is it flat? The function isn’t changing at that point, therefore it's **constant**.

## **Increasing and Decreasing Intervals**

An **interval** is just a stretch of the graph where something happens. When you’re looking at a graph:

* It’s **increasing** when the graph goes up as you move from left to right.
* It’s **decreasing** when the graph goes down as you move from left to right.

**Helpful Tip:** Always read graphs from left to right, just like you read a book!

## **Intercepts (aka Where the Graph Crosses the Axes)**

* The **y-intercept** is where the graph touches the **y-axis**. This tells you what the function equals when ![LaTeX: x=0](https://k12.instructure.com/equation_images/x%253D0?scale=1 "x=0"). Think of it as the function’s **starting point** in many real-life situations.
* The **x-intercepts** are where the graph touches the **x-axis**. These are also called zeros of the function, because this is when ![LaTeX: f\left(x\right)=0](https://k12.instructure.com/equation_images/f%255Cleft(x%255Cright)%253D0?scale=1 "f\left(x\right)=0"). In real life, these could represent when something runs out, hits the ground, or stops happening.

## **Using Tables to Understand a Function**

Sometimes you’ll be given a table of values instead of a graph. Tables are another great way to represent a function—you can see how the values of x and y are related.

### **Look for Patterns!**

* If ![LaTeX: y](https://k12.instructure.com/equation_images/y?scale=1 "y") increases by the same amount each time ![LaTeX: x](https://k12.instructure.com/equation_images/x?scale=1 "x") increases, you’re likely looking at a **linear function**.
* If the pattern changes, you might be seeing something **nonlinear**, like a quadratic or exponential function.

### **Helpful Hints**

1. **Always read left to right.** That’s how graphs are meant to be understood.
2. **Label everything!** Intercepts, intervals, points—marking them helps you see the big picture.
3. **Talk it out.** Describe what’s happening in your own words: “As x increases, y decreases.”
4. **Use color coding** when sketching graphs or analyzing tables. Highlight increases in green and decreases in red.
5. **Practice translating between tables, graphs, and verbal descriptions.** The more you switch between formats, the stronger your understanding becomes.

### **Key Vocabulary**

| Word | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Function** | A rule that assigns exactly one output to each input |
| **x-intercept** | Where the graph crosses the x-axis (y = 0) |
| **y-intercept** | Where the graph crosses the y-axis (x = 0) |
| **Increasing** | The graph goes up as you move to the right |
| **Decreasing** | The graph goes down as you move to the right |
| **Constant** | The graph stays flat (no change in y-values) |
| **Interval** | A range of numbers between two points |
| **Rate of Change** | The rate at which one quantity is changing with respect to another quantity |

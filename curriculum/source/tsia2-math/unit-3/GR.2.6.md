# GR.2.6 - Volume of 3D Figures

**Topic ID:** GR.2.6  
**Unit:** 3  
**Strand:** GR (Geometric & Spatial Reasoning)  
**Assessment Layer:** CRC  
**Author:** Juan Dolores Oviedo  

---

#### **Learning Objectives**

- Calculate volume as base area times height for prisms and cylinders, correctly squaring the radius for circular bases.
- Apply the one-third factor for cones and pyramids and the four-thirds $\pi r^{3}$ formula for spheres, without dropping, doubling, or misapplying the fraction.
- Calculate the volume of composite solids and partially filled uniform containers, and solve backward for a missing height or radius from a given volume.

---

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Filling, Not Wrapping

GR.2.5 asked how much paper wraps a solid. This asks how much **fits inside**. Volume is measured in cubic units, because it multiplies three lengths together rather than summing flat pieces.

For anything with a uniform cross-section, the idea is one sentence: **area of the base, times the height.**

$$V = (\text{base area}) \times h$$

- Rectangular prism: base is a rectangle, so $V = lwh$. A $6$ by $4$ by $3$ box holds $72$.
- Cylinder: base is a circle, so $V = \pi r^{2} h$.

**Example 1:** A cylinder with radius $5$ and height $4$.

$$V = \pi r^{2} h = 3.14 \times 25 \times 4 = 314$$

The radius is **squared** because it belongs to the base's area, which is $\pi r^{2}$. Using $3.14 \times 5 \times 4 = 62.8$ forgets that and computes something that is not a volume at all.

The figure shows a cone with a radius of $3$ and a perpendicular height of $8$, dimensioned on a line beside the solid.

A cone is **not** a cylinder. It is exactly one third of the cylinder that would enclose it.

$$V = \frac{1}{3}\pi r^{2} h = \frac{1}{3}(3.14)(9)(8) = \frac{226.08}{3} = 75.36$$

Answering $226.08$ gives the whole enclosing cylinder, three times too much. The same one-third applies to a pyramid, for the same reason: it comes to a point.

$$\text{cone} = \frac{1}{3} \times \text{cylinder} \qquad \text{pyramid} = \frac{1}{3} \times \text{prism}$$

**One third, not one half.** Halving instead gives $113.04$, which is a common near miss because halving feels like the natural way to shrink something. The factor is a third, and it is worth remembering it comes from the shape tapering to a point rather than from anything about halves.

And do not apply it twice. Dividing $75.36$ by $3$ again reaches $25.12$, a ninth of the cylinder.

The figure shows a sphere with a radius of $6$ drawn from the marked centre, with a dashed ellipse showing the equator seen at an angle.

$$V = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$$

The radius is **cubed** here, not squared, because a sphere has no separate height to multiply by.

$$\frac{4}{3}(3.14)(216) = \frac{4}{3}(678.24) = 904.32$$

Compare with the sphere's surface area, $4\pi r^{2} = 4(3.14)(36) = 452.16$. Same radius, different power, different units. **The exponent tells you which one you computed:** squared is a covering, cubed is a filling.

##### The Whole Family

| Solid | Volume |
|---|---|
| rectangular prism | $lwh$ |
| cylinder | $\pi r^{2} h$ |
| cone | $\frac{1}{3}\pi r^{2} h$ |
| pyramid | $\frac{1}{3}(\text{base area})h$ |
| sphere | $\frac{4}{3}\pi r^{3}$ |

**Example 2:** A square pyramid with base edge $6$ and height $9$.

- Base area: $6^{2} = 36$
- Volume: $\frac{1}{3}(36)(9) = 108$

Note that the pyramid's volume uses the **perpendicular height**, not the slant height that its surface area needed in GR.2.5. Two formulas for the same solid, each wanting a different length.

---

##### Composite Solids and Part-Filled Ones

**Example 3:** A cylinder of radius $3$ and height $10$, with a cone of radius $3$ and height $4$ on top.

- Cylinder: $3.14(9)(10) = 282.6$
- Cone: $\frac{1}{3}(3.14)(9)(4) = 37.68$
- Total: $320.28$

**Example 4:** A cylindrical tank of radius $2$ and height $5$, filled halfway.

- Full: $3.14(4)(5) = 62.8$
- Half: $31.4$

Filling to half the height halves the volume, because the base area never changes as you go up. That is only true for a shape with a uniform cross-section, which is why it works for a cylinder and would not for a cone.

---

##### Running It Backward

**Example 5:** A cylinder has volume $502.4$ and radius $4$. Find its height.

$$\pi r^{2} h = 502.4 \quad \rightarrow \quad 3.14(16)h = 502.4 \quad \rightarrow \quad 50.24h = 502.4 \quad \rightarrow \quad h = 10$$

Divide by the **whole** base area, $\pi r^{2}$, not by $\pi r$. Dividing by $3.14 \times 4$ gives $40$, four times too big, which is the squaring error showing up in reverse.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Dropping the one-third.** A cone or pyramid is a third of the solid that encloses it.
2. **Using one-half instead, or applying the third twice.**
3. **Forgetting to square the radius.** It belongs to the base's area.
4. **Confusing volume with surface area.** Squared is a covering; cubed, or an area times a height, is a filling.

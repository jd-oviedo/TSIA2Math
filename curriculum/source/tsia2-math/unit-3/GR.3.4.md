#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Nothing New, Except the Sentence

Everything you need is already in GR.3.1 through GR.3.3. What changes here is that nobody hands you a triangle. You are given a sentence about a ladder or a lighthouse and you have to build the triangle yourself.

The whole difficulty of this topic is in three steps, and only the first is new:

1. **Draw the triangle and label what the words gave you.**
2. Pick the relationship that uses the side you have and the side you want.
3. Solve, then ask whether the answer could really be that size.

Skipping step one is what makes these problems feel hard. They are not harder than GR.3.3. They are the same problems with the picture removed.

The figure shows the triangle for an angle-of-elevation problem. The observer stands at the lower right, $50$ ft of level ground runs to the base of the object, the angle of elevation is $32^\circ$, and the unknown height $h$ rises from the far end. The height is drawn at $50$ times the tangent of $32^\circ$, so a little under two thirds the ground distance.

An **angle of elevation** is measured up from the horizontal. An **angle of depression** is measured down from the horizontal. Both are measured from a level line, never from a vertical one, and that single fact settles which side is opposite and which is adjacent.

$$\tan 32^\circ = \frac{h}{50} \quad \rightarrow \quad h = 50(0.625) = 31.2 \text{ ft}$$

The ground distance is **adjacent** to the angle. The height is **opposite** it. Handing back $50$ answers "how far away is it", which was not the question.

The figure shows a ladder against a wall. The ground runs $6$ ft from the wall to the foot of the ladder, the ladder reaches $8$ ft up the wall, and the ladder itself is $10$ ft. The legs are drawn in a true $6$ to $8$ proportion.

The ladder is always the **hypotenuse**, the wall is always **vertical**, and the ground is always **horizontal**. Once you know that, a ladder problem is either a Pythagorean problem or a trigonometry problem depending on whether you were given an angle.

$$6^{2} + 8^{2} = 100, \qquad \sqrt{100} = 10$$

**Example 1:** A $13$ ft ladder with its foot $5$ ft from the wall.

$$13^{2} - 5^{2} = 169 - 25 = 144, \qquad \sqrt{144} = 12$$

Subtract when you are looking for a **leg**, add when you are looking for the **hypotenuse**. And the lengths themselves never combine directly: $13 - 5$ is $8$, which is not the answer, because the theorem is about squares.

##### The Question Behind the Question

Many of these problems ask for a **change**, not a position. Read the last sentence twice.

**Example 2:** A balloon is at $200$ ft. It rises until, from a point $150$ ft away horizontally, its angle of elevation is $60^\circ$. **How far did it rise?**

$$\text{new height} = 150(1.732) = 259.8$$

$$\text{rise} = 259.8 - 200 = 59.8 \text{ ft}$$

The trigonometry gives $259.8$, and $259.8$ is a **height**, not a rise. Handing it back leaves the whole second half of the problem undone.

And subtract in the right direction. $200 - 259.8$ is $-59.8$, and a balloon cannot rise a negative distance. **A negative answer to a "how far" question is always wrong**, so it is a free check.

---

##### Adding a Height You Were Given

**Example 3:** A surveyor's eye is $5$ ft above the ground, $60$ ft from a flagpole. From her eye the angle of elevation to the top is $40^\circ$.

$$\text{above eye level} = 60(0.839) = 50.3$$

$$\text{pole} = 50.3 + 5 = 55.3 \text{ ft}$$

The triangle sits on the line of the surveyor's eye, not on the ground, so the trigonometry only ever gives you the part **above** that line. The $5$ ft underneath it has to be put back by hand.

---

##### Two Sightings of the Same Object

**Example 4:** From the top of a lighthouse $80$ ft above the water, the angles of depression to two boats directly in line are $30^\circ$ and $45^\circ$. How far apart are the boats?

Do it as two separate triangles that share the same height of $80$.

- Nearer boat: $\tan 45^\circ = \frac{80}{d_{1}}$, so $d_{1} = \frac{80}{1} = 80$
- Farther boat: $\tan 30^\circ = \frac{80}{d_{2}}$, so $d_{2} = \frac{80}{0.577} = 138.6$
- Apart: $138.6 - 80 = 58.6$ ft

Two things to hold on to. **The smaller angle belongs to the farther object**, so $d_{2}$ has to come out bigger than $d_{1}$, and if it does not you have divided the wrong way.

And the angles do not combine. Adding them to $75^\circ$ and running one calculation gives $\frac{80}{3.732} = 21.4$, which is not a distance in this problem at all. **There is no single triangle here.** There are two, and each one has to be solved on its own.

---

##### When the Answer Is an Area

**Example 5:** A ramp rises at $30^\circ$ over a horizontal run of $24$ ft. Find the area of its triangular side panel.

- Height: $24(0.577) = 13.85$
- Area: $\frac{1}{2}(24)(13.85) = 166.2$ square ft

Trigonometry gives you the missing **length**. The area formula still needs its one half, and dropping it doubles the answer to $332.4$.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Answering the wrong question.** A height is not a rise; a distance is not a height.
2. **Forgetting an offset.** An eye height or a starting altitude has to be added or subtracted by hand.
3. **Subtracting backwards.** A negative distance is never the answer.
4. **Combining two angles.** Two sightings mean two triangles.
5. **Reporting the hypotenuse.** The line of sight and the ladder are not the height.

**1. A ladder makes a $60^\circ$ angle with the ground and its foot is $5$ ft from the wall. How high up the wall does it reach?**

Step 1: Draw it. The ground of $5$ is **adjacent** to the $60^\circ$ angle, the wall height is **opposite** it, and the ladder is the hypotenuse.

Step 2: Opposite over adjacent is tangent.
- $\tan 60^\circ = \frac{h}{5}$, so $h = 5(1.732) = 8.66$, which rounds to $8.7$

Step 3: Check. $60^\circ$ is a steep ladder, so it should reach higher than its foot is out, and $8.7 > 5$.

**Answer: A** ($8.7$)

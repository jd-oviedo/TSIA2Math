#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Five Formulas and One Idea

Area is how much surface a figure covers, measured in square units. Five formulas cover almost everything on the test.

| Figure | Area |
|---|---|
| rectangle | $A = bh$ |
| triangle | $A = \frac{1}{2}bh$ |
| parallelogram | $A = bh$ |
| trapezoid | $A = \frac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)h$ |
| circle | $A = \pi r^{2}$ |

The one idea underneath them: a parallelogram is a rectangle with a corner slid over, so it has the same area. A triangle is half a parallelogram. A trapezoid is a parallelogram whose two parallel sides differ, so it uses their **average** length, which is what the $\frac{1}{2}(b_1 + b_2)$ is.

That is worth knowing because it tells you where each $\frac{1}{2}$ comes from and therefore when it belongs.

The figure shows a parallelogram with a base of $9$ and a slanted side of $5$. The dashed line inside it drops straight down from the top edge to the base, meeting it at a right angle, and that dashed length of $4$ is the **height**.

$$A = bh = 9 \times 4 = 36$$

Using the $5$ gives $45$, which is too big. The slanted side is longer than the height precisely because it leans, and the area does not care how far the shape leans.

**The height is always perpendicular to the base.** Look for the right-angle mark, or for the word "perpendicular", or for a dashed line drawn inside the figure. A side length printed along a slanted edge is there to be rejected.

This applies to triangles and trapezoids in exactly the same way.

##### Where the One-Half Belongs

Three errors cluster here and they are worth separating.

- **Dropping it.** A triangle with base $10$ and height $6$ has area $\frac{1}{2}(10)(6) = 30$, not $60$. Sixty is the parallelogram it is half of.
- **Applying it twice.** Halving $30$ again to get $15$ usually happens when the formula is written down and then the halving is done a second time by hand.
- **Borrowing it.** A rectangle has **no** $\frac{1}{2}$. Using $\frac{1}{2}bh$ on a rectangle halves an answer that was already right.

**Say which figure you have before you pick a formula**, and the halving takes care of itself.

##### Area Is Not Perimeter, and Not Circumference

A rectangle $12$ by $5$ has area $60$ square units and perimeter $34$ units. They are different quantities in different units, and the question always says which it wants.

The same split applies to circles. Radius $6$ gives

$$A = \pi r^{2} = 3.14 \times 36 = 113.04 \qquad C = 2\pi r = 3.14 \times 12 = 37.68$$

**The square is the tell.** Area squares a length, so it carries square units. If your circle answer has no squaring in it, you computed a distance.

---

##### Composite Figures

Break the shape into pieces you have formulas for, then add. If something is removed, subtract.

**Example 1:** A rectangle $12$ by $8$ with a triangle of base $12$ and height $5$ sitting on top.

- Rectangle: $12 \times 8 = 96$
- Triangle: $\frac{1}{2}(12)(5) = 30$
- Total: $96 + 30 = 126$

**Example 2:** A rectangle $10$ by $8$ with a $3$ by $2$ rectangle cut out of it.

- Whole: $80$
- Cutout: $6$
- Remaining: $80 - 6 = 74$

**A cutout is subtracted, never added.** Adding it gives $86$, describing more material than the uncut rectangle had.

---

##### Running It Backward

**Example 3:** A circle has area $78.5$. Find its radius.

$$\pi r^{2} = 78.5 \quad \rightarrow \quad r^{2} = \frac{78.5}{3.14} = 25 \quad \rightarrow \quad r = 5$$

Two steps, and the second is the one that gets skipped. Stopping at $25$ reports $r^{2}$, not $r$. **Undo the square as well as the $\pi$.**

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Using a slanted side as the height.** The height is perpendicular; look for the right-angle mark or the dashed line.
2. **Mishandling the one-half.** Dropping it, doubling it, or importing it into a rectangle.
3. **Adding a cutout instead of subtracting it.**
4. **Stopping at $r^{2}$.** Take the square root to finish.

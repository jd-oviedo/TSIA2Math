#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### What Similar Buys You

GR.4.2 established when two polygons are similar. This topic is about what you can then work out.

Two polygons are similar when

- every pair of corresponding **angles** is equal, and
- every pair of corresponding **sides** is in the same ratio.

That common ratio is the **scale factor**, written $k$. Once you have $k$, every length in the figure is settled.

The figure shows two similar right triangles drawn to the same scale. The smaller has sides $3$, $4$ and $5$; the larger has sides $9$, $12$ and $15$. Every side of the larger is exactly three times the corresponding side of the smaller.

$$k = \frac{\text{a side of the new figure}}{\text{the corresponding side of the old one}}$$

$$k = \frac{9}{3} = \frac{12}{4} = \frac{15}{5} = 3$$

All three pairs give the same number. **If they do not, the polygons are not similar**, and checking a second pair is the cheapest way to catch a bad pairing.

**Direction matters, and the fraction is where it lives.** Going from the small triangle to the large one, $k = 3$. Going the other way, $k = \frac{1}{3}$. Both are correct answers to different questions, so read which figure the question starts from.

**Example 1:** The $3$-$4$-$5$ triangle scaled by $3$. Its longest side becomes

$$5 \times 3 = 15$$

Multiply going up, divide coming down. Answering $\frac{5}{3}$ has the direction backwards, and the answer is smaller than what you started with even though the figure got bigger.

---

##### Perimeter Follows the Sides

A perimeter is a sum of lengths, so it scales exactly the way a length does.

$$\frac{\text{new perimeter}}{\text{old perimeter}} = k$$

**Example 2:** A polygon with a perimeter of $10$ scaled by $k = 4$.

$$10 \times 4 = 40$$

Nothing subtle happens here. The trap is importing the area rule and multiplying by $16$.

The figure shows two similar rectangles drawn to the same scale. The smaller measures $5$ by $2$; the larger measures $12.5$ by $5$, so $k = 2.5$. Their areas are $10$ and $62.5$.

$$\frac{62.5}{10} = 6.25 = 2.5^{2}$$

$$\frac{\text{new area}}{\text{old area}} = k^{2}$$

An area is two lengths multiplied together, and **both** of them scaled. That is where the square comes from, and it is worth understanding rather than memorising, because the rule then tells you which way it goes.

| Quantity | Scales by |
|---|---|
| any side | $k$ |
| perimeter | $k$ |
| area | $k^{2}$ |

**Example 3:** $k = 4$, and the smaller polygon has an area of $10$.

$$10 \times 4^{2} = 10 \times 16 = 160$$

Answering $40$ scaled the area by $k$, which is the single most common error in this topic.

---

##### Working Backwards From Areas

If you are given areas and want a length ratio, take a **square root**.

**Example 4:** Two similar polygons have areas of $16$ and $81$.

$$\frac{k^{2}}{1} = \frac{81}{16}, \qquad k = \sqrt{\frac{81}{16}} = \frac{9}{4}$$

So corresponding sides are in the ratio $\frac{4}{9}$ from the smaller to the larger. Handing back $\frac{16}{81}$ reports an area ratio where a length ratio was asked for.

**Example 5:** Two similar polygons have perimeters of $30$ and $90$, and the smaller has an area of $50$.

- Perimeters give the length ratio directly: $k = \frac{90}{30} = 3$
- Areas take the square: $50 \times 3^{2} = 450$

Note the route. **Perimeters give you $k$ in one step; areas make you take a root first.** Reading a perimeter ratio as though it needed a root, or an area ratio as though it did not, is the whole difficulty here.

---

##### A Check That Always Works

Before you write the answer down, ask whether it moved in the right direction.

- Going to a **bigger** figure, every length, perimeter and area must **increase**.
- Going to a **smaller** figure, all of them must **decrease**.

An answer that shrinks when the figure grew means you divided where you should have multiplied, or wrote the scale factor upside down.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Scaling an area by $k$** instead of $k^{2}$.
2. **Scaling a length by $k^{2}$** instead of $k$.
3. **Writing the scale factor upside down**, giving $\frac{1}{k}$.
4. **Dividing where you should multiply**, so a bigger figure comes out smaller.

**7. Two similar triangles have perimeters of $30$ and $90$. The smaller has an area of $50$. What is the area of the larger?**

Step 1: Perimeters are lengths, so their ratio **is** $k$, with no root needed.
- $k = \frac{90}{30} = 3$

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Symmetry Is a Transformation That Changes Nothing

GR.4.1 and GR.4.2 moved figures around. A figure has **symmetry** when one of those moves puts it back exactly where it started.

- **Line symmetry:** a reflection maps the figure onto itself.
- **Rotational symmetry:** a rotation of less than a full turn maps the figure onto itself.

They are different properties, and a figure can have one without the other. Counting one when you were asked for the other is the commonest error here, so read which word the question used.

The figure shows a regular hexagon with all six of its axes drawn as dashed lines through the centre. Three pass through pairs of opposite vertices; three pass through pairs of opposite edge midpoints.

**A regular polygon with $n$ sides has exactly $n$ lines of symmetry.** A hexagon has $6$, not $3$ and not $12$.

Two ways to get that wrong, and both are worth naming.

**Counting each axis twice.** A single dashed line runs all the way across the figure. It is **one** axis, not two, even though it has two ends. Counting the ends gives $12$, which is twice the truth.

**Missing half of them.** For an even-sided polygon the axes come in two kinds, vertex to vertex and midpoint to midpoint. Finding only the vertex ones gives $3$ and stops halfway.

For an **odd**-sided polygon there is only one kind of axis, because no vertex has a vertex opposite it. Five sides, five axes. The count is still $n$.

##### Order and Angle

Rotational symmetry has two numbers, and they are not the same number.

- The **order** is how many times the figure lands on itself in one full turn.
- The **angle** is the smallest rotation that works.

$$\text{order} = n \qquad \text{smallest angle} = \frac{360^\circ}{n}$$

For a regular pentagon: order $5$, angle $72^\circ$. For a regular octagon: order $8$, angle $45^\circ$.

**Order is a count. The angle is measured in degrees.** Answering $72$ when the question said "order" hands back the wrong quantity, and the giveaway is that an order can never be a number like $72$ for a five-sided figure.

Going backwards works the same way. Given a smallest angle of $24^\circ$:

$$n = \frac{360}{24} = 15$$

so $15$ sides, $15$ lines of symmetry, order $15$.

---

##### Point Symmetry

A figure has **point symmetry** when a rotation of exactly $180^\circ$ maps it onto itself. Turn it upside down and it looks unchanged.

For a regular polygon there is a one-line test:

$$\text{point symmetry} \iff n \text{ is even}$$

A $180^\circ$ turn is half a full turn, so it only lands on a position the figure occupies when the order divides evenly in two.

| Figure | Point symmetry? |
|---|---|
| square, $n = 4$ | yes |
| regular hexagon, $n = 6$ | yes |
| regular octagon, $n = 8$ | yes |
| equilateral triangle, $n = 3$ | no |
| regular pentagon, $n = 5$ | no |
| regular nonagon, $n = 9$ | no |

**Not every regular polygon has point symmetry.** Turn an equilateral triangle $180^\circ$ and it points the other way, which is a different position. Assuming regularity is enough is a genuine trap, and the parity test settles it in a second.

---

##### Figures That Are Not Regular

The two symmetries really do come apart, and these are the cases that show it.

| Figure | Lines | Order | Point symmetry |
|---|---|---|---|
| square | $4$ | $4$ | yes |
| rectangle, not a square | $2$ | $2$ | yes |
| parallelogram, neither | $0$ | $2$ | yes |
| isosceles trapezoid | $1$ | $1$ | no |
| circle | infinitely many | infinite | yes |

Read the middle two rows together. A parallelogram has **no** line of symmetry at all and still turns onto itself at $180^\circ$. An isosceles trapezoid has **one** line of symmetry and no rotational symmetry whatever.

Order $1$ means the only rotation that works is the full turn, which every figure has. **Order $1$ is the same as no rotational symmetry.**

The rectangle's two axes run through the midpoints of opposite sides. Its diagonals are **not** axes: fold along one and the corners do not meet.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Answering the wrong property.** Lines and order are different counts.
2. **Counting each axis twice**, once from each end.
3. **Finding some axes and missing the rest.**
4. **Assuming every regular polygon has point symmetry.** Only the even-sided ones do.

**9. Which statement about a regular polygon with an odd number of sides is true?**

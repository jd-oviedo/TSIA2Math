#### **Learning Objectives**

- Translate "less than" and "subtracted from" phrases into expressions with the reversed subtraction order they require.
- Group phrases like "sum of," "difference of," or "quantity" in parentheses before applying an outside operation.
- Write an expression for a flat fee plus a per-unit rate, attaching the variable to the quantity that repeats.

---

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### English Into Symbols

This topic asks you to turn a sentence into an expression. There is no arithmetic in it at all, which is exactly why students underestimate it and lose points.

Start with the moves that are safe, because most of the words behave.

| Words | Operation |
|---|---|
| sum, plus, more than, increased by, total | $+$ |
| difference, minus, decreased by, take away | $-$ |
| product, times, twice, of | $\times$ |
| quotient, per, divided by, ratio of | $\div$ |

Those you can read left to right and be right. **Two phrases cannot**, and they are the whole difficulty of the topic.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

"Less than" and "subtracted from" run **backward**.

Write "5 less than a number $n$."

Reading left to right gives $5 - n$. That is wrong. The answer is $n - 5$.

Test it with numbers, which is the move that settles every version of this question. What is 5 less than 12? It is 7. Now check the candidates:

- $n - 5$ gives $12 - 5 = 7$. Correct.
- $5 - n$ gives $5 - 12 = -7$. Not the answer.

**"Less than" tells you what to take away, not what to start with.** You start with the thing it is less *than*.

Same for "subtracted from": "3 subtracted from $x$" is $x - 3$, because $x$ is the thing being subtracted from.

Note what does **not** reverse: "less" on its own, and "decreased by." "A number decreased by 5" is $n - 5$, read straight through. It is specifically the phrase "less **than**" that flips.

Whenever you meet one of these, plug in a friendly number and check. It takes five seconds and it is the only defence you need.

---

##### Parentheses Are Not Optional

The second big idea is that some words create a **group**, and a group needs parentheses.

The signal words are **sum**, **difference**, **total** and **quantity**. When one of those is followed by "of," everything in that phrase gets bundled before anything else touches it.

**Example 1:** "Twice the sum of a number and 6."

The sum of a number and 6 is $n + 6$. That whole thing gets doubled:

$$2(n + 6)$$

Writing $2n + 6$ doubles only the $n$ and leaves the 6 alone. Check with $n = 4$: the sum is 10, twice that is 20. But $2(4) + 6 = 14$. Different expression, different answer.

**Example 2:** "Three times the sum of a number and 5, decreased by 2."

Build it in the order the sentence gives you.

- The sum of a number and 5: $n + 5$
- Three times that: $3(n + 5)$
- Decreased by 2: $3(n + 5) - 2$

The comma matters. It closes the group before "decreased by 2" arrives, so the 2 sits outside the parentheses. Had the sentence read "three times the sum of a number and 5 decreased by 2," the 2 would belong inside: $3(n + 5 - 2)$.

**Read to the end of the sentence before you write anything down.** Where the phrase stops decides where the parenthesis closes.

---

##### Flat Fee Plus a Rate

This pattern is everywhere on the test, in this topic and again in QR.4.1.

**Example 3:** A gym charges a \$40 joining fee plus \$25 per month. Write the cost for $m$ months.

$$25m + 40$$

The \$25 is a **rate**: it happens once per month, so it rides on the variable. The \$40 is a **flat fee**: it happens once, full stop, so it stands alone.

The error worth naming is merging them into $65m$. That charges the joining fee every single month. Test it at $m = 1$: both give \$65, which is why the error survives. Test at $m = 2$: the correct expression gives $50 + 40 = 90$, and $65m$ gives 130. The fee got charged twice.

**Test a flat-fee expression at two different values, never one.** At $m = 1$ every wrong version of this looks right.

The mirror error is writing $40m + 25$, which swaps which number rides the variable. Ask which quantity repeats. Months repeat, so the per-month charge is the one with $m$ attached.

---

##### Two Quantities From One Variable

**Example 4:** The length of a rectangle is 3 feet more than twice its width $w$. Write the perimeter.

Do this in two stages. First name both sides in terms of the one variable.

- Width: $w$
- Length: $2w + 3$

Then build what was asked. Perimeter is two widths and two lengths:

$$2(w) + 2(2w + 3) = 2w + 4w + 6 = 6w + 6$$

The trap is the second 2 not reaching the 3. Writing $2w + 2 \cdot 2w + 3 = 6w + 3$ doubles the $2w$ and forgets the $+3$ was inside the length as well. **Every term of the length gets doubled, because the whole side appears twice.**

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Reading "less than" forward.** "5 less than $n$" is $n - 5$. Plug in a number and check.
2. **Dropping the parentheses.** "Sum," "difference" and "quantity" create a group that must be bundled before anything multiplies it.
3. **Merging a flat fee into the rate.** \$40 plus \$25 per month is $25m + 40$, never $65m$. Test at two values.
4. **Attaching the variable to the wrong number.** The quantity that repeats is the one that rides the variable.

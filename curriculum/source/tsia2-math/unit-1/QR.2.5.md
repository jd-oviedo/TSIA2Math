#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### A Rate Is a Ratio With Units Attached

Miles per gallon. Dollars per ounce. Pages per minute. Every one of them is a division, and the word **per** tells you which number goes on top.

"Miles per gallon" means miles $\div$ gallons. The quantity named first goes in the numerator. That is not a convention you have to memorize separately; it is what the phrase says.

A car goes 252 miles on 12 gallons.

$$\frac{252 \text{ miles}}{12 \text{ gallons}} = 21 \text{ miles per gallon}$$

A **unit rate** is a rate whose second number is 1. That is what makes rates so useful: once you know the value of one, you can scale to any amount by multiplying.

**Read the "per" phrase, then divide in that order.** If the answer comes out looking strange, you probably divided backward, and that is easy to test: 21 miles per gallon is a sensible car. $0.048$ gallons per mile is the same fact upside down, and it is not what was asked.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

You average two rates by adding them and halving.

A driver goes 120 miles at 60 mph, then another 120 miles at 40 mph. What is the average speed for the whole trip?

Everyone says 50. It is 48.

Here is why. Average speed is not the average of the speeds. It is

$$\text{average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}$$

and you have to compute both totals before you divide.

- First leg: $120 \div 60 = 2$ hours.
- Second leg: $120 \div 40 = 3$ hours.
- Totals: 240 miles in 5 hours, so $240 \div 5 = 48$ mph.

The reason it is not 50 is worth sitting with. The driver spent **3 hours** going slowly and only **2 hours** going fast. More of the trip happened at the low speed, so the average has to be pulled below the midpoint. Averaging the two numbers pretends equal time was spent at each, and that was never true.

**You can only average two rates directly when the thing underneath them is equal.** Equal distances do not make it work. Equal times do.

Test that claim: 2 hours at 50 and 3 hours at 70 gives $100 + 210 = 310$ miles in 5 hours, which is 62 mph, not the 60 you would get from averaging. Again pulled toward the speed that got more time.

---

##### Comparing With Unit Rates

**Example 1:** A 12-ounce box of cereal costs \$4.20. What is the price per ounce?

$$\frac{4.20}{12} = 0.35$$

35 cents per ounce. Now this box can be compared with any other box of any size, which is the whole point of a unit rate: it strips out the size difference so only the value is left.

The dangerous slip here is the decimal. $\frac{4.20}{12}$ is $0.35$, not $3.50$. A quick check: 12 ounces at \$3.50 each would be \$42, not \$4.20. **Multiply your unit rate back by the count and see whether you land on the total you started with.**

---

##### Scaling With a Unit Rate

**Example 2:** A worker earns \$96 for 8 hours. What is the pay for 14 hours?

- Unit rate: $96 \div 8 = 12$ dollars per hour.
- Scale: $14 \times 12 = 168$.

Two moves, divide then multiply, and the order matters. Multiplying first gives $96 \times 14 = 1344$, which is what you would earn in 112 hours.

And do not stop at the 12. The unit rate is the tool, not the answer, which is the same warning as QR.2.2 and it does not get less true here.

---

##### Working Together

**Example 3:** Pump A fills a tank in 6 hours. Pump B fills the same tank in 3 hours. Working together, how long?

The wrong answer is 9 hours, and it is wrong in a way you can see instantly: **adding two helpers cannot make a job take longer than either of them alone.** Pump B does it in 3 hours by itself. Together they must beat 3.

Times do not add. **Rates add.** So convert to rates, add, then convert back.

- Pump A: $\frac{1}{6}$ of the tank per hour.
- Pump B: $\frac{1}{3}$ of the tank per hour.
- Together: $\frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}$ of the tank per hour.

Half a tank per hour means the whole tank takes 2 hours.

Check it against the sanity rule: 2 is less than 3. Good.

You can also work in real units when the problem gives you a size. A tap fills a 90-liter tub in 12 minutes, so $\frac{90}{12} = 7.5$ liters per minute. A second tap takes 36 minutes, so $\frac{90}{36} = 2.5$ liters per minute. Together $7.5 + 2.5 = 10$ liters per minute, and $90 \div 10 = 9$ minutes. Same method, friendlier numbers.

---

##### Closing Rates

**Example 4:** Two trains are 300 miles apart and move toward each other at 70 mph and 50 mph. How far does the faster train travel before they meet?

The gap closes at the **sum** of the speeds, because both are working on it: $70 + 50 = 120$ mph.

- Time to meet: $300 \div 120 = 2.5$ hours.
- The faster train travels $70 \times 2.5 = 175$ miles.

Check: the slower train covers $50 \times 2.5 = 125$, and $175 + 125 = 300$. The whole gap is accounted for.

Two things to guard. The 120 is a **rate**, not a distance, and writing "120 miles" is the standard wrong answer. And the 2.5 is a **time**; the question asked how far. Watch your units at every step, because this problem hands you three correct numbers and only one of them answers it.

Toward each other, add. Same direction, subtract, exactly as in QR.2.2.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Averaging two speeds.** Total distance over total time. The leg that took longer counts for more.
2. **Dividing backward.** Read the "per" phrase and put the first-named quantity on top.
3. **Adding times for work done together.** Rates add, times do not, and the answer must beat the faster worker alone.
4. **Answering with the rate or the time when the question asked for a distance.** Check units before you bubble.

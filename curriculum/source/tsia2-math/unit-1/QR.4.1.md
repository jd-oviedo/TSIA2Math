#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Where the Unit Lands

This is the last topic in the unit, and it does not teach a new technique. Every tool it uses came from somewhere earlier: unit rates from QR.2.5, flat-fee-plus-rate from QR.3.1, scaling from QR.2.1, conversions from QR.2.6.

What is new is that **the problem no longer tells you which tool to use.** It gives you a situation and a question, and picking the model is the work.

So the method here is about reading, not computing.

**Step 1:** Ask whether the relationship is genuinely proportional. Does doubling the input double the output?  
**Step 2:** If something happens once and does not repeat, it is a flat amount and sits outside the proportion.  
**Step 3:** If different amounts are charged at different rates, it is tiered, and each tier is its own calculation.  
**Step 4:** Answer the question that was asked, in the units it asked for.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

You apply one rate to the whole amount when the problem set up two.

An electric utility charges \$0.12 per kWh for the first 500 kWh and \$0.18 per kWh beyond that. What is the bill for 800 kWh?

The tempting move is $800 \times 0.18 = 144$, using the rate the problem finished on. Or $800 \times 0.12 = 96$, using the one it started with. Both are wrong, and both are wrong in the same way: **a tiered problem is not one multiplication, it is one multiplication per tier, added.**

Split the 800 at the boundary the problem gave you.

- First 500 kWh at \$0.12: $500 \times 0.12 = 60$
- The remaining 300 kWh at \$0.18: $800 - 500 = 300$, and $300 \times 0.18 = 54$
- Total: $60 + 54 = 114$

The dangerous part is the second line. **The higher rate applies to 300 kWh, not to 800.** Getting that subtraction right is most of the item, and writing down "how many units fall in this tier" before multiplying anything is what keeps it straight.

A related error is charging the *second* tier's rate on the first tier's units, which gives $500 \times 0.18 + 300 \times 0.12 = 90 + 36 = 126$. Same arithmetic, rates swapped. **Match each rate to its own band before you multiply.**

---

##### Flat Amounts Sit Outside

**Example 1:** A delivery service charges a \$6 base fee plus \$1.50 per mile. What is the charge for a 14-mile delivery?

- Per-mile part: $1.50 \times 14 = 21$
- Base fee: \$6, once
- Total: $21 + 6 = 27$

Two errors, mirror images, both of which feel complete. Answering \$21 drops the base fee. Answering \$6 drops the mileage. And merging them into \$7.50 per mile gives $7.50 \times 14 = 105$, charging the base fee fourteen times.

**Write both pieces down before you add.** This is the QR.3.1 habit and it does not stop being useful.

---

##### First-Unit Pricing

**Example 2:** A parking garage charges \$5 for the first hour and \$3 for each hour after. What is the cost of 4 hours?

This is a tiered problem wearing different clothes. The first hour is its own tier.

- First hour: \$5
- Remaining hours: $4 - 1 = 3$ hours at \$3, which is \$9
- Total: \$14

The error is the count, not the rates: charging \$3 for all four hours gives $5 + 12 = 17$ and pays for five hours of parking. **The word "after" means you subtract before you multiply.**

---

##### Rates Apply to the Whole, Not Just the New Part

**Example 3:** A savings account holds \$800 and earns $5\%$ interest for the year. A \$200 deposit is made at the start of the year. How much interest is earned?

The deposit arrives at the start, so it earns interest for the full year just like the rest.

- Balance: $800 + 200 = 1000$
- Interest: $0.05 \times 1000 = 50$

Applying the $5\%$ only to the new \$200 gives \$10, and applying it only to the original \$800 gives \$40. Both are half an answer.

**Find the quantity the rate acts on before you apply the rate.** Here that is the whole balance, and the two-step order matters: combine first, then take the percent.

---

##### Chaining Through a Context

The hardest items in this topic are not conceptually hard. They are long, and they punish you for stopping early.

**Example 4:** A blueprint uses a scale of 1 inch to 8 feet. A wall measures 3.5 inches on the blueprint and is 10 feet tall. Paint covers 40 square feet per gallon. How many gallons are needed?

Four quantities, one chain. Take it a link at a time and label every result.

- Actual length: $3.5 \times 8 = 28$ feet
- Wall area: $28 \times 10 = 280$ square feet
- Paint: $280 \div 40 = 7$ gallons

Every intermediate number is real and none of them is the answer. 28 is a length, 280 is an area, and only 7 is a count of gallons.

**Write the unit next to every number as you go.** When the unit next to your last number matches the unit in the question, you are done, and not before.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **One rate for a tiered problem.** Split at the boundary, multiply each band separately, add.
2. **Miscounting the units in a tier.** "Beyond 500" means the total minus 500. "After the first hour" means the total minus 1.
3. **Losing the flat amount, or multiplying it.** It happens once and stands outside the rate.
4. **Stopping mid-chain.** Label every intermediate with its unit and compare against the question.

Step 1: Check that it is proportional. Twice the people needs twice the rice, so it is.

Step 2: Find the unit rate.

Step 3: Scale to 10 people.
- $10 \times 0.75 = 7.5$ cups

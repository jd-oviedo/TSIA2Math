# AR.2.1 - Solving One-Variable Linear Equations

**Topic ID:** AR.2.1  
**Unit:** 2  
**Strand:** AR (Algebraic Reasoning)  
**Assessment Layer:** CRC  
**Author:** Juan Dolores Oviedo  

---

#### **Learning Objectives**

- Solve a one-variable linear equation by undoing operations with their correct inverse, in reverse order.
- Distribute correctly across every term inside parentheses before combining like terms.
- Distinguish equations with no solution from those with infinitely many solutions when the variable cancels.

---

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### A Balance Scale With a Covered Box

Picture an old balance scale, level. On the left sits a box whose contents you cannot see, plus a $9$ pound weight. On the right sits a $14$ pound weight. It balances.

What is in the box?

You worked that out already: $5$ pounds. And notice how you did it. You took $9$ pounds off the left, and to keep the scale level you took $9$ pounds off the right too.

That is the whole method, and it never changes:

**Whatever you do to one side of an equation, do to the other side. Peel away everything around the variable until it stands alone.**

$$x + 9 = 14 \quad \longrightarrow \quad x + 9 - 9 = 14 - 9 \quad \longrightarrow \quad x = 5$$

The scale stayed balanced the entire time. That is why the answer is trustworthy.

---

##### Every Operation Has an Undo

To peel something away, apply its opposite.

| The equation does this to $x$ | You undo it with |
|---|---|
| adds a number | subtract that number |
| subtracts a number | add that number |
| multiplies by a number | divide by that number |
| divides by a number | multiply by that number |

**Example 1:** Solve $3x = 21$.

The equation multiplies $x$ by $3$. The undo is division by $3$.

- $\frac{3x}{3} = \frac{21}{3}$
- $x = 7$

Check: $3(7) = 21$. Correct.

The most common wrong move here is to **subtract** the $3$, giving $21 - 3 = 18$. But the $3$ is not being added to $x$, it is multiplying $x$. Subtraction does not undo multiplication. Look at what the coefficient is *doing* before you decide how to remove it.

---

##### Order Matters: Undo in Reverse

When a variable has more than one thing wrapped around it, peel in reverse order. Get rid of the **added or subtracted** number first, then deal with the coefficient.

**Example 2:** Solve $2x + 5 = 13$.

Step 1: Subtract $5$ from both sides. Constants come off first.
- $2x = 8$

Step 2: Divide both sides by $2$.
- $x = 4$

Step 3: Check. $2(4) + 5 = 8 + 5 = 13$. Correct.

Now the wrong order, so you can see exactly what it costs. A student who divides first writes $\frac{13}{2} = 6.5$ and is stuck with a decimal that never appears in the real answer. The $5$ was still attached, so dividing by $2$ divided the $5$ along with everything else.

**Constants off first. Coefficient second.** Say it as a rhythm.

---

##### Subtracted Constants Get Added Back

**Example 3:** Solve $4x - 6 = 10$.

Step 1: The equation **subtracts** $6$, so undo it by **adding** $6$ to both sides.
- $4x = 16$

Step 2: Divide by $4$.
- $x = 4$

Step 3: Check. $4(4) - 6 = 16 - 6 = 10$. Correct.

Step 1 is the whole item. A student who subtracts $6$ from both sides gets $4x = 4$ and $x = 1$, which fails the check: $4(1) - 6 = -2$, not $10$. The sign of the constant tells you which way to move it, and the check catches you when you get it wrong.

---

##### The Variable Can Start on the Right

**Example 4:** Solve $7 = 2x + 1$.

Nothing is different. The equals sign does not care which side you read first.

Step 1: Subtract $1$ from both sides.
- $6 = 2x$

Step 2: Divide by $2$.
- $3 = x$, so $x = 3$

Step 3: Check. $2(3) + 1 = 7$. Correct.

If having the variable on the right bothers you, flip the whole equation first and write $2x + 1 = 7$. That is always legal.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

Read this section twice.

**When parentheses are in the way, distribute to every term inside, or divide the whole side.**

**Example 5:** Solve $4(x + 3) = 28$.

There are two correct routes.

**Route 1, divide first.** The left side is $4$ times a group, so divide both sides by $4$.
- $x + 3 = 7$
- $x = 4$

**Route 2, distribute first.** Multiply the $4$ into **both** terms.
- $4x + 12 = 28$
- $4x = 16$
- $x = 4$

Same answer, as it must be. Check: $4(4 + 3) = 4(7) = 28$. Correct.

Here is the error. A student who distributes to the first term only writes $4x + 3 = 28$, then $4x = 25$, then $x = 6.25$. The $4$ touched the $x$ and never touched the $3$.

**The number outside the parentheses multiplies every term inside.** Draw two arrows if it helps.

The other trap on this item is Route 1 stopping too early. Dividing gives $x + 3 = 7$, and a student who reports $7$ has answered a question about $x + 3$, not about $x$. One more step to go.

---

##### When the Variable Is Divided

**Example 6:** Solve $\frac{x}{2} + 3 = 7$.

Step 1: Constants first. Subtract $3$.
- $\frac{x}{2} = 4$

Step 2: The equation divides $x$ by $2$, so undo it by **multiplying** both sides by $2$.
- $x = 8$

Step 3: Check. $\frac{8}{2} + 3 = 4 + 3 = 7$. Correct.

Step 2 is where students slip. Seeing a $2$ underneath, the reflex is to divide again, giving $x = 2$. But $2$ fails the check: $\frac{2}{2} + 3 = 4$, not $7$. A denominator is undone by multiplying.

---

##### Variables on Both Sides

Collect all the variable terms on one side and all the constants on the other. Move the smaller variable term to keep the coefficient positive.

**Example 7:** Solve $5x - 4 = 3x + 10$.

Step 1: Subtract $3x$ from both sides, since $3x$ is the smaller variable term.
- $2x - 4 = 10$

Step 2: Add $4$ to both sides.
- $2x = 14$

Step 3: Divide by $2$.
- $x = 7$

Step 4: Check both sides separately with $x = 7$.
- Left: $5(7) - 4 = 35 - 4 = 31$
- Right: $3(7) + 10 = 21 + 10 = 31$
- They match, so $x = 7$ is right.

Step 4 is the strongest check in the topic. Two independent computations landing on the same number is nearly impossible by accident.

Watch the constant in Step 2. The equation has $-4$ on the left, so it gets **added** to both sides. A student who subtracts instead reaches $2x = 6$ and $x = 3$, which fails: $5(3) - 4 = 11$ but $3(3) + 10 = 19$.

---

##### When the Variables Cancel Completely

Sometimes every variable term vanishes. What is left tells you the answer.

**Example 8:** Solve $3x + 5 = 3x - 2$.

Step 1: Subtract $3x$ from both sides.
- $5 = -2$

Step 2: Read what remains. Is $5$ equal to $-2$? No. That statement is simply false, and no value of $x$ can repair it, because $x$ is gone.

**Answer: no solution.**

Think about it on the scale. Both sides carry the same number of boxes. The left also carries $5$ pounds and the right carries a $2$ pound deficit. No box weight will ever level it.

The mirror case looks nearly identical and means the opposite:

$$3x + 5 = 3x + 5 \quad \longrightarrow \quad 5 = 5$$

That statement is **true**, and it is true no matter what $x$ is. **Answer: all real numbers.**

So the rule for when the variable disappears:

- What remains is **false** ($5 = -2$): **no solution**.
- What remains is **true** ($5 = 5$): **all real numbers**.

Do not answer $x = 0$ here. The variable did not turn out to be zero, it left the problem entirely. Those are different claims.

---

##### Parentheses on Both Sides

**Example 9:** Solve $2(3x - 5) = 4(x + 1) - 2$.

Step 1: Distribute on each side, into every term.
- Left: $2(3x) - 2(5) = 6x - 10$
- Right: $4(x) + 4(1) - 2 = 4x + 4 - 2$

Step 2: Simplify the right side before going further.
- $4x + 4 - 2 = 4x + 2$
- The equation is now $6x - 10 = 4x + 2$

Step 3: Subtract $4x$ from both sides.
- $2x - 10 = 2$

Step 4: Add $10$ to both sides.
- $2x = 12$

Step 5: Divide by $2$.
- $x = 6$

Step 6: Check both sides with $x = 6$.
- Left: $2(3 \cdot 6 - 5) = 2(18 - 5) = 2(13) = 26$
- Right: $4(6 + 1) - 2 = 4(7) - 2 = 28 - 2 = 26$
- Match.

Step 2 is worth noting. Tidying each side completely before moving anything across the equals sign keeps the work short. Students who start shuffling terms across too early end up with six terms in play and lose a sign.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Subtracting a coefficient instead of dividing.** In $3x = 21$, the answer is $7$, not $18$. Multiplication is undone by division.
2. **Dividing before removing the constant.** In $2x + 5 = 13$, subtract the $5$ first. Dividing first gives $6.5$ and it is wrong.
3. **Moving a constant the wrong way.** In $4x - 6 = 10$, the $6$ gets **added** to both sides. Its sign tells you the direction.
4. **Distributing to the first term only.** $4(x + 3)$ is $4x + 12$, never $4x + 3$.
5. **Answering the middle step.** Dividing $4(x + 3) = 28$ gives $x + 3 = 7$. That is not $x$ yet. Reread the question.

Every one of these is caught by substituting your answer back in. When you miss a problem below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.

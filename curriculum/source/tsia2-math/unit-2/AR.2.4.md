# AR.2.4 - Solving Systems of Linear Equations

**Topic ID:** AR.2.4

#### **Learning Objectives**

- Solve a system of two linear equations using substitution or elimination to find the pair satisfying both.
- Verify a candidate solution by checking it in both equations, not just one.
- Identify a system as having no solution, one solution, or infinitely many by comparing full converted equations.

---

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### One Pair That Works in Both

A system is two equations at once, and its solution is the pair $(x, y)$ that makes **both** true. Not one. Both.

$$y = 2x - 1 \qquad y = -x + 5$$

Each equation on its own has endless solutions: pick any $x$ and it gives you a $y$. The system asks which single pair the two agree on.

Set the two expressions for $y$ equal, because at the solution they describe the same number.

$$2x - 1 = -x + 5$$
$$3x = 6$$
$$x = 2$$

Then find $y$ from either equation: $2(2) - 1 = 3$. Check it in the **other** one: $-(2) + 5 = 3$. Agrees, so the solution is $(2, 3)$.

The graph says the same thing geometrically. The line $y = 2x - 1$ climbs steeply and the line $y = -x + 5$ falls; they cross at exactly one point, $(2, 3)$, and that crossing point is the solution. Every other point on either line satisfies only its own equation.

**Write the answer as an ordered pair, $x$ first.** Reporting $(3, 2)$ is the most common way to lose a question you actually solved.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

You check your candidate in one equation and stop.

Is $(4, 0)$ a solution of $2x - y = 8$ and $x + 2y = 9$?

Test the first: $2(4) - 0 = 8$. True. It is tempting to stop there, and the point looks confirmed.

Test the second: $4 + 2(0) = 4$, which is not 9. False.

So $(4, 0)$ is **not** a solution. It sits on the first line only. A point on one line and not the other is exactly what a non-solution looks like, and there are infinitely many of them, so satisfying one equation is no evidence at all.

**Both equations, every time.** This is the cheapest check in the topic and it catches every wrong candidate the test offers.

---

##### Two Methods

**Substitution** works best when one equation already has a variable alone.

$$y = 4x - 3 \qquad 2x + y = 9$$

Replace $y$ in the second equation with what the first says it equals:

$$2x + (4x - 3) = 9 \quad \rightarrow \quad 6x = 12 \quad \rightarrow \quad x = 2, \; y = 5$$

Substitute the expression into the **other** equation. Putting $4x - 3$ back into the equation it came from gives $4x - 3 = 4x - 3$, which is true for everything and tells you nothing.

**Elimination** works best when a variable has matching or opposite coefficients.

$$3x + y = 14 \qquad 2x - y = 6$$

The $+y$ and $-y$ cancel when you add the equations:

$$5x = 20 \quad \rightarrow \quad x = 4$$

Then $3(4) + y = 14$ gives $y = 2$. Check in the second: $2(4) - 2 = 6$. Correct, so $(4, 2)$.

Notice that $5x = 20$ is not the answer, and neither is 20. **Finish the division, then find the second variable, then check.**

---

##### When There Is No Single Answer

Two lines can also miss each other or lie on top of each other, and the algebra tells you which by what it collapses to.

**No solution.** $y = 3x + 2$ and $y = 3x - 5$.

Setting them equal gives $3x + 2 = 3x - 5$, so $2 = -5$. That is false, and no $x$ can fix it. The lines have the same slope and different intercepts, so they are parallel and never meet.

**Infinitely many solutions.** $2x + 3y = 12$ and $4x + 6y = 24$.

The second equation is exactly twice the first. They are the same line written two ways, so every point on it satisfies both. Solving gives $0 = 0$, which is true for everything.

| What the algebra gives | What it means |
|---|---|
| $x = $ a number | one solution, the lines cross |
| a false statement like $2 = -5$ | no solution, parallel lines |
| a true statement like $0 = 0$ | infinitely many, the same line twice |

**Check for a multiple before you conclude anything.** Proportional equations look different and are not: $4x + 6y = 24$ is $2x + 3y = 12$ doubled, and reading that as parallel is the standard error. Compare the ratios of every coefficient, constants included. If all three match, it is one line; if the variable ratios match but the constant does not, it is parallel.

And when there are infinitely many solutions, naming a single point like $(3, 2)$ is not the answer. That point works, but so does every other point on the line.

##### Systems From Words

**Example 1:** Two numbers sum to 30 and differ by 8. Find them.

$$x + y = 30 \qquad x - y = 8$$

Adding eliminates $y$: $2x = 38$, so $x = 19$ and $y = 11$.

Check both: $19 + 11 = 30$ and $19 - 11 = 8$. Correct.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Checking one equation only.** A candidate must satisfy both.
2. **Swapping the coordinates.** Write $x$ first.
3. **Stopping at $5x = 20$.** Finish the division, then find the other variable.
4. **Reading a multiple as parallel.** Compare all three coefficient ratios before deciding.

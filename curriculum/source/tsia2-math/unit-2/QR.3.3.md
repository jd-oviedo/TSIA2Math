# QR.3.3 - Creating a Two-Variable Expression From a Situation

**Topic ID:** QR.3.3  
**Unit:** 2  
**Strand:** QR (Quantitative Reasoning)  
**Assessment Layer:** CRC  
**Author:** Juan Dolores Oviedo  

---

#### **Learning Objectives**

- Write a two-variable cost expression with one rate term per quantity, each attached to its own variable.
- Distinguish "per" quantities, which need a variable, from "once" flat charges, which don't.
- Translate comparative relationships like "3 times as many X as Y" into a correct equation, checking with a number.

---

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Two Things Being Counted at Once

QR.3.1 built expressions with one variable. This topic adds a second, and the method does not change: **each quantity gets its own rate, and the rates never mix.**

Apples cost \$3 each and pears cost \$5 each. You buy $a$ apples and $p$ pears.

$$3a + 5p$$

Read it back one term at a time. $3a$ is what the apples cost. $5p$ is what the pears cost. Add them and you have the bill.

The structure is always the same:

| Piece | Looks like | Repeats? |
|---|---|---|
| first item's cost | rate $\times$ its own variable | once per item |
| second item's cost | rate $\times$ its own variable | once per item |
| flat charge | a bare number | never |

**Write one term per quantity, then check that every rate is sitting on the variable it belongs to.**

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

You merge the two rates into one.

Apples \$3, pears \$5, so students write $8(a + p)$: eight dollars for every piece of fruit.

That is only correct when you buy the same number of each, and the problem never says you do. Test it. Buy 4 apples and 1 pear. The truth is $3(4) + 5(1) = 17$. The merged version gives $8(5) = 40$.

**Two different rates cannot become one rate.** They are prices for different things, and the whole reason there are two variables is that the counts can differ.

Two relatives of this error to watch for:

- **Dropping the rates entirely**, writing $a + p$. That counts pieces of fruit, not dollars. Check your units: the question asked for a cost, and $a + p$ is a count.
- **Swapping the rates**, writing $5a + 3p$. Each number is right and each is on the wrong variable. Reread the sentence and match them one at a time.

---

##### Flat Charges Sit Outside

A store sells notebooks at \$2 and pens at \$1.50, and adds a \$4 delivery fee to every order.

$$2n + 1.5p + 4$$

The \$4 has no variable because it does not repeat. Same rule as QR.3.1, now with two rate terms in front of it instead of one.

The error to name here is turning a **per-unit** price into a flat one: writing $2 + 1.5p + 4$, which charges \$2 for notebooks no matter how many you buy. If a price is quoted "each" or "per," it must ride a variable.

**Every "per" gets a variable. Every "once" does not.**

---

##### Comparative Relationships

Some problems do not give you two independent counts. They tie one to the other, and then the whole thing collapses to a single variable.

**Example 1:** There are 3 times as many students as teachers. With $t$ teachers, how many students?

$$s = 3t$$

This reverses in students' hands constantly, producing $t = 3s$. Test with a number. If there are 10 teachers, there are 30 students. Does $s = 3t$ give that? $3(10) = 30$. Yes. Does $t = 3s$? It would say $10 = 3(30) = 90$. No.

**Put a number in.** The sentence "3 times as many students as teachers" means the students are the big group, so the 3 multiplies the small one.

Watch the other misreading too: "3 times as many" is multiplication, not $s = t + 3$.

**Example 2:** A shop sells small bags at \$4 and large bags at \$7, and sells 5 more large bags than small. With $s$ small bags, what is the revenue?

Name the second quantity first: large bags are $s + 5$.

$$4s + 7(s + 5) = 4s + 7s + 35 = 11s + 35$$

The parentheses are load-bearing. Writing $4s + 7s + 5$ adds five dollars instead of five bags, and those five extra bags are worth \$7 each, so the constant should be $35$.

**When a quantity is described in terms of another, write it in brackets and distribute.**

---

##### A Percentage on the Whole Thing

**Example 3:** A caterer charges \$18 per adult and \$10 per child, then adds a $20\%$ service charge on the food total. With $a$ adults and $c$ children:

$$1.2(18a + 10c)$$

The service charge applies to the whole food total, so the whole food total goes in brackets. Dropping them gives $1.2 \times 18a + 10c = 21.6a + 10c$, which charges the service fee on the adults only.

**Whatever a percentage applies to, bracket it first, then multiply.**

---

##### Reading an Expression Back

Half the items in this topic run the other direction: you are given an expression and asked what it means.

$$T = 6b + 9c + 25$$

- $6$ is attached to $b$, so each bracelet is \$6.
- $9$ is attached to $c$, so each chain is \$9.
- $25$ has no variable, so it is charged once per order, not per item.

**A number with a variable is a "per." A number without one is a "once."** That single sentence answers most interpretation items in this topic.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Merging two rates.** \$3 apples and \$5 pears is not \$8 per fruit. Test with unequal counts.
2. **Swapping the rates.** Match each price to its own variable, one at a time.
3. **Forgetting the brackets.** 5 more large bags is $7(s+5)$, which adds \$35, not \$5.
4. **Reversing a comparison.** "3 times as many students as teachers" is $s = 3t$. Put a number in to check.

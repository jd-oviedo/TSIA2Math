#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Two Questions, Asked in Order

More studying goes with higher scores. Colder weather goes with higher heating bills. You already reason about relationships between two quantities constantly.

A **scatterplot** puts that reasoning on a grid: one variable across the bottom, the other up the side, and one dot per observation. Then it asks you two questions, and you answer them in this order:

1. **Direction.** As $x$ goes up, does $y$ tend to go **up**, **down**, or **neither**?
2. **Strength.** How **tightly** do the points hug a single straight line?

Those two questions are independent. Direction is about which way, strength is about how closely. Confusing one for the other is the main way this topic is failed, so keep them in separate compartments from the start.

A note on how this lesson is written. A scatterplot is a picture, and this lesson has no pictures in it, so every plot below is either **described in words** or **given as a table of points**. That is exactly how these items are worded on the test as well, so the practice transfers. When you meet a described plot, sketch it roughly on scratch paper. Six dots on the corner of a page is enough.

---

##### Direction

**Positive association:** as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to increase. The cloud of points runs uphill from left to right.

**Negative association:** as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to decrease. The cloud runs downhill from left to right.

**No linear association:** as $x$ increases, $y$ does not tend to go either way. The cloud is a shapeless blob or a flat horizontal band.

Read those two words carefully: **tends to**. A positive association does **not** require every single point to be higher than the one before it. It describes the overall drift.

**Example 1:** A researcher records study hours and test scores. As study hours increase, test scores tend to increase. What is the association?

Uphill, so **positive**. Nothing more to compute.

**Example 2:** As average outdoor temperature increases, spending on home heating decreases. What is the association?

Uphill in $x$, downhill in $y$, so **negative**.

Notice that a negative association is a real, strong, useful relationship. **Negative does not mean weak or absent.** It means the two quantities move in opposite directions, which is often the most valuable thing you could know.

---

##### Reading Direction From a Table

When you are handed points instead of a picture, walk the $y$ values in order and watch which way they drift.

**Example 3:** What is the association for these points?

| $x$ | $y$ |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $4$ |
| $2$ | $6$ |
| $3$ | $9$ |
| $4$ | $11$ |
| $5$ | $14$ |

Step 1: Read the $y$ column top to bottom: $4, 6, 9, 11, 14$.

Step 2: Every step goes up, so as $x$ rises, $y$ rises.

Step 3: The association is **positive**.

Now the trap that this item exists to catch. The steps up are $+2$, $+3$, $+2$, $+3$. They are **not equal**, and a student may conclude there is no linear association because the data are not perfectly straight.

That reasoning is wrong. **Real data are never perfectly straight.** A linear association means the points cluster around a line, not that they sit exactly on one. If exact equality were required, no real dataset would ever qualify and the whole topic would be empty.

**Example 4:** And these?

| $x$ | $y$ |
|---|---|
| $1$ | $20$ |
| $2$ | $17$ |
| $3$ | $15$ |
| $4$ | $12$ |
| $5$ | $9$ |

The $y$ values fall throughout: **negative** association. The steps are $-3$, $-2$, $-3$, $-3$, again not identical, and again that changes nothing.

---

##### No Linear Association

**Example 5:** A study compares students' shoe sizes with their history exam scores. Plotted, the points scatter with no overall upward or downward tilt.

**No linear association.** Shoe size tells you nothing about the exam score, which is what you would have guessed before seeing any data.

**Example 6:** A plot has student ID number across the bottom and quiz score up the side. The points sit in a roughly horizontal band centered near $75$, with no tilt.

**No linear association.** A flat band is the signature. As ID number increases, the score does not tend to move at all, which makes sense because an ID number is an arbitrary label.

Two things that do **not** create an association:

- Both variables being measured in positive numbers.
- The plot having many points.

A relationship has to show up as a **tilt** in the cloud. No tilt, no linear association.

---

##### Strength Is About Tightness, Not Steepness

Now the second question, and the source of the topic's hardest items.

**Strength** measures how closely the points cluster around a single straight line.

- **Strong:** the points sit very close to a line. Little scatter.
- **Weak:** the points drift in a direction but are widely spread out.

**Example 7:** A plot of hours practiced against free throws made rises from about $(1, 8)$ to about $(11, 46)$, and the points lie very close to a single straight line with almost no scatter.

Direction: uphill, positive. Strength: tight, so strong. Answer: **strong positive**.

**Example 8:** A plot of daily steps against hours of sleep drifts gently upward but the points are widely scattered, with no tight line anywhere.

Direction: uphill, positive. Strength: loose, so weak. Answer: **weak positive**.

Examples 7 and 8 have the **same direction** and completely different strengths. That is the proof that the two questions are independent, and it is why you answer them separately.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

Read this section twice.

**Strength is not steepness, and strength is not direction.**

Three false beliefs, all common, all worth dismantling explicitly.

**"Steeper means stronger."** No. Steepness is about the slope of the trend; strength is about the scatter around it. A gently rising line with points sitting right on it is a **strong** association. A steeply rising cloud with points flung far from the line is a **weak** one. Ask how far the points sit from the line, not how tilted the line is.

**"Positive is stronger than negative."** No. Direction and strength are unrelated. A tight downhill line is a **strong negative** association, and it is every bit as strong as the equivalent uphill one. A negative slope says nothing whatever about strength.

**"Points not exactly on a line means no association."** No. That is the Example 3 trap. Scatter around a line is normal and expected.

**Example 9:** A plot of outdoor temperature against cups of hot chocolate sold has points clustered tightly along a line that falls from left to right.

Direction: downhill, negative. Strength: tight, so strong. Answer: **strong negative**.

**Example 10:** Two plots use the same axes, study hours against test score. In Plot 1 the points lie very close to a straight upward line. In Plot 2 they also trend upward but are widely scattered. Which is stronger?

**Plot 1**, because its points lie closer to a straight line. Both are positive, so direction cannot break the tie. Tightness does, every time.

---

##### Curves Are Not Linear

A pattern can be strong, obvious, and completely real while still having **no linear** association.

**Example 11:** A plot of average daily temperature against monthly energy bill forms a clear U-shape. Bills are high near $\$200$ at low temperatures, drop to about $\$80$ near $65$ to $70$ degrees, then climb back to about $\$200$ at high temperatures.

There is a strong relationship here, and the mechanism is obvious: you heat the house when it is cold and cool it when it is hot. But the association is **not linear**. It goes down and then up, so no single straight line describes it, and no single direction label fits.

The correct answer is: **no linear association, though a clear curved pattern exists.**

Both tempting wrong answers miss for the same reason. Calling it strong negative describes only the left half. Saying it is "linear but switches direction at the midpoint" is a contradiction, because a line does not switch direction; that is what makes it a line.

**Example 12:** A plot of years of experience against annual salary rises steeply at first and then flattens out as experience grows, bending as it climbs.

Direction: upward throughout, so **positive**. But it is a curve, not a line, so the honest description is **a positive association that is not linear**.

Compare Examples 11 and 12 carefully. The U-shape has **no** direction, because it goes both ways. The flattening curve has a **positive** direction throughout, it just is not straight. Different answers, and the distinction is which one you are looking at.

---

##### Outliers Can Manufacture a Trend

A small number of extreme points can create, hide, or weaken an apparent relationship.

**Example 13:** A plot of screen time against sleep shows most points on a clear downward trend, but one point sits in the upper right at about $(9, 9)$: high screen time **and** high sleep, against the trend. What happens to the association if that point is removed?

The remaining points follow the downward trend more tightly, so removing it **strengthens the negative association**.

The reasoning that generalizes: a point that contradicts the trend is holding the association back. Remove it and what is left is cleaner. A point that **follows** the trend is doing the opposite, and removing it would weaken the association.

**Example 14:** A plot of pets owned against monthly grocery spending has most points in a flat, trendless cloud near $\$400$, but two extreme points stand apart: one near $(0, 220)$ and one near $(8, 760)$. Together those two create the visual impression of an upward line.

The honest answer: **little to no linear association, and the apparent upward trend is driven by two extreme points.**

This is the most important idea in the topic, because it is the one that matters outside a classroom. **Ask whether the trend is supported by the bulk of the data or by a couple of extreme points.** If covering two dots with your thumb makes the relationship disappear, the relationship was those two dots.

---

##### What a Scatterplot Cannot Tell You

Two limits worth knowing, both of which appear as answer choices.

**Association is not causation.** Points trending together does not establish that one variable causes the other. Ice cream sales and drowning deaths rise together, and neither causes the other; hot weather drives both.

**You do not need a correlation coefficient to name a direction.** If a plot or a description shows the points drifting upward, the association is positive, and that is a complete answer. A choice saying "the direction cannot be determined without calculating the correlation coefficient" is offering a false requirement. The coefficient puts a precise number on strength; direction is readable off the plot.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Confusing strength with direction.** A tight downhill line is strong negative. Negative says nothing about strength.
2. **Judging strength by steepness.** Strength is how close the points sit to the line, not how tilted the line is.
3. **Demanding a perfect line.** Unequal steps between points are normal. Association means clustering around a line.
4. **Forcing a direction onto a curve.** A U-shape has no linear association even though its pattern is obvious.
5. **Trusting a trend built from two points.** Ask whether the bulk of the data supports it.

When you miss one below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.

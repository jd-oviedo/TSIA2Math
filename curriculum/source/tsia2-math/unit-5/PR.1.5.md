#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Two Questions, Asked in Order

Before you can display data you have to know what kind of data it is, and the test asks you two questions in that order:

1. **Is the variable a name or a number?** A name puts each subject in a group. A number measures or counts something about it.
2. **If it is a number, does it count or measure?** Counting gives whole values with nothing in between. Measuring gives a value anywhere on a scale.

**The confusion this topic exists to prevent is treating a number as numerical just because it is written with digits.** A jersey number, a zip code and a room number are all digits and none of them is a quantity. Adding two zip codes gives nothing.

The test of a numerical variable is not how it is written. It is whether arithmetic on it means anything.

---

##### Categorical or Numerical

A variable is **categorical** when its values are labels that sort subjects into groups: favourite season, hair colour, brand of phone, yes or no.

A variable is **numerical** when its values are quantities you can meaningfully compare and combine: height, number of absences, temperature, price.

**Example 1:** A survey asks students to name their favourite season. Is "favourite season" categorical or numerical?

Step 1: List some values.
- Spring, Summer, Fall, Winter.

Step 2: Ask whether arithmetic on them means anything.
- There is no "average season". Spring plus Summer is not a season.

Step 3: Classify.
- **Categorical.**

**The digits test:** if you can write the values as words with no loss, the variable is categorical. If you can rank or average the values and the result means something, it is numerical.

---

##### Discrete or Continuous

Within numerical, there are two kinds.

- **Discrete** values come from counting. They are whole and separate, with nothing in between. Number of absences, number of siblings, cars in a car park.
- **Continuous** values come from measuring. Between any two values there is another one. Weight, height, time, temperature.

**Example 2:** A scientist records the exact weight in grams of soil samples. Which kind is "weight"?

Step 1: Counting or measuring?
- Measuring, on a balance.

Step 2: Can a value sit between two others?
- Yes. Between $4.1$ and $4.2$ grams there is $4.15$.

Step 3: Classify.
- Numerical and **continuous**.

**Example 3:** A teacher records the number of absences per student.

Step 1: Counting.

Step 2: Between $3$ and $4$ absences there is nothing. Half an absence is not a value this variable takes.

Step 3: Numerical and **discrete**.

The word "exact" in a stem is a signal for measuring. The words "number of" are a signal for counting.

---

##### Choosing a Display

The display follows the variable type. It is not a matter of taste.

- **Categorical variable, showing how many in each group:** a **bar graph**. The bars are separate because the categories are separate.
- **Numerical variable, showing the shape of a distribution:** a **histogram**. The bars touch because the values run continuously along the axis.
- **One numerical variable over time:** a **line graph**.
- **Two numerical variables, looking for a relationship:** a **scatterplot**.
- **Parts of one whole:** a **circle graph**.

**Example 4:** A student collects hair colour for $30$ classmates and wants to show how many have each colour. Which display?

Step 1: Classify the variable.
- Hair colour is categorical.

Step 2: The question is how many in each group.

Step 3: Choose.
- A **bar graph**.

A histogram would be wrong, and not slightly. A histogram's touching bars claim the categories sit next to each other on a scale, and hair colours do not.

---

##### Summarising Can Delete the Thing You Were Studying

**Example 5:** A researcher wants to show how much daily rainfall **varies** across a month. Someone suggests reporting the mean daily rainfall. What is wrong with that?

Step 1: What is being studied?
- The variation from day to day.

Step 2: What does a mean report?
- One number for the whole month.

Step 3: Compare.
- The mean is the same for a month of identical drizzle and a month with one storm. **Averaging is exactly the operation that removes variation**, so it cannot display it.

To show variation you need something that keeps the individual values: a line graph over the month, a histogram of daily totals, or a box plot.

---

##### What a Sample Can and Cannot Tell You

A sample describes the group it came from. Extending it to a wider group is a separate claim that needs the sample to be representative of that wider group.

Thirty classmates describe those thirty classmates. They do not describe the school, the district or teenagers in general, and no amount of care in collecting them changes that.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

**Deciding the display before classifying the variable.** The display question feels like the real question, so it gets answered first, and then the variable type is chosen to fit the display already picked.

Do it in the given order. Classify first, in words, out loud: "hair colour is categorical". Then the display follows with almost no thought. Reversed, every wrong display becomes defensible, because you can always tell a story about why a graph might work.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Digits read as numerical.** Zip codes and jersey numbers are labels. Ask whether arithmetic on them means anything.
2. **Counting read as measuring.** "Number of" is discrete; nothing sits between $3$ and $4$.
3. **A histogram used for categories.** Touching bars claim a continuous scale that categories do not have.
4. **Averaging to show variation.** The mean is what removes variation; it cannot display it.
5. **A sample described as the population.** Thirty classmates describe thirty classmates.

**Basic Level** (try these first)

1. A survey asks students to name their favourite season: Spring, Summer, Fall or Winter. Which type of variable is "favourite season"?
   - A) Categorical, because its values are labels that sort students into groups
   - B) Numerical and discrete, because there are exactly four seasons to count
   - C) Numerical and continuous, because a year runs continuously through the seasons
   - D) Numerical, because the seasons can be put in calendar order

2. A scientist records the exact weight in grams of $20$ soil samples. Which type of variable is "weight of a soil sample"?
   - A) Categorical, because each sample is given its own label
   - B) Numerical and discrete, because there are $20$ separate samples
   - C) Numerical and continuous, because weight can take any value on a scale
   - D) Numerical and discrete, because the weights are recorded to the nearest gram

3. A teacher records the number of absences for each student. Which type of variable is "number of absences"?
   - A) Numerical and continuous, because a student could be absent for part of a day
   - B) Numerical and discrete, because absences are counted in whole units
   - C) Categorical, because each count sorts students into a group
   - D) Numerical, and whether it is discrete or continuous depends on the class size

4. Each player on a team is given a jersey number from $1$ to $25$. Which type of variable is "jersey number"?
   - A) Numerical and discrete, because the numbers are whole and separate
   - B) Numerical and continuous, because the numbers run from $1$ to $25$
   - C) Categorical, because the numbers are labels and arithmetic on them means nothing
   - D) Numerical, because the mean jersey number can be computed

**Proficient Level** (these require an extra step)

5. A student collects the hair colour of $30$ classmates and wants to display how many have each colour. Which display is appropriate?
   - A) A histogram, because it shows how the $30$ classmates are distributed
   - B) A scatterplot, because it shows the relationship between colour and count
   - C) A line graph, because it shows how the colours change across the class
   - D) A bar graph, because hair colour is categorical and the bars stay separate

6. A researcher measures the height of $200$ adults and wants to show the shape of the distribution. Which display is appropriate?
   - A) A histogram, because height is continuous and the bars sit along a scale
   - B) A bar graph, because each height is its own category
   - C) A circle graph, because it shows each height as a part of the whole
   - D) A line graph, because height increases steadily across the group

7. A shop records its daily total sales for $30$ days and wants to show how much the daily total **varies** across the month. Which approach is appropriate?
   - A) Report the mean daily sales, because one number summarises the month
   - B) Report the number of days, because that is what varies
   - C) Draw a line graph of the daily totals, because it keeps every day's value
   - D) Report the mean and the number of days together, because two numbers say more than one

**Advanced Level** (these need multiple steps or reverse thinking)

8. A student surveys $30$ classmates and finds that $18$ prefer online homework. Which conclusion is appropriate?
   - A) Most students at the school prefer online homework
   - B) $18$ of the $30$ classmates surveyed prefer online homework
   - C) Most teenagers prefer online homework
   - D) Preference for online homework is a continuous variable, since $18$ out of $30$ is a proportion

9. A researcher wants to see whether hours studied is related to test score, for $40$ students. Which display is appropriate?
   - A) A circle graph of hours studied, because it shows parts of a whole
   - B) Two bar graphs side by side, because there are two variables
   - C) A histogram of test scores, because scores are numerical
   - D) A scatterplot, because two numerical variables are being compared

10. A student says: "Any variable recorded with digits is numerical, and any variable recorded with words is categorical." Is that rule correct?
    - A) Yes, because how the data is written is what determines its type
    - B) No, because digits can be labels: a zip code is written with digits and arithmetic on it means nothing
    - C) Yes, but only for discrete variables, since continuous ones are always digits
    - D) No, because words can be numerical whenever they name an amount

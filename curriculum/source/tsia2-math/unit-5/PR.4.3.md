# PR.4.3 - Calculating Percent Change From Data Over Time

**Topic ID:** PR.4.3  
**Unit:** 5  
**Strand:** PR (Probabilistic and Statistical Reasoning)  
**Assessment Layer:** CRC  
**Author:** Juan Dolores Oviedo  

---

#### **Learning Objectives**

- Calculate percent change as (new - original) divided by the original value, always dividing by the starting value.
- Use the sign of the change to correctly label a result as an increase or a decrease.
- Compound successive percent changes by multiplying ratios rather than adding the percentages.

---

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Two Questions, Asked in Order

Percent change asks two questions, and the order is the whole topic:

1. **How much did it change?** New minus original. Keep the sign; it tells you the direction.
2. **Change compared with what?** Always the **original** value. Never the new one.

**The confusion this topic exists to prevent is dividing by the wrong number.** Both values are sitting in the stem, both give a clean-looking percent, and only one of them is the base. Dividing by the new value is the single most common wrong answer on this topic, and it is wrong every time except when nothing changed.

$$\text{percent change} = \frac{\text{new} - \text{original}}{\text{original}} \times 100$$

---

##### The Base Is the Starting Value

**Example 1:** A price rises from \$40 to \$50. What is the percent increase?

Step 1: The change.
- $50 - 40 = 10$

Step 2: Divide by the **original**, which is $40$.
- $10 / 40 = 0.25$

Step 3: Convert to a percent.
- $0.25 \times 100 = 25$, so $25\%$.

Dividing by $50$ instead gives $10 / 50 = 0.2$, which is $20\%$. That is a real number, it is on the page, and it answers a question nobody asked: what fraction of the **new** price the rise was.

**Say it before you divide: "compared with what it started at."**

---

##### The Change Is Not the Percent

**Example 2:** The same rise, \$40 to \$50.

The change is $10$. The percent change is $25\%$. These are different quantities in different units, and $10$ is not an answer to "what percent".

A rise of $10$ on a base of $40$ is $25\%$. The same rise of $10$ on a base of $200$ is $5\%$. **The change alone cannot tell you the percent, because it does not know the base.**

---

##### New Over Original Is Not the Change

**Example 3:** Still \$40 to \$50.

- $50 / 40 = 1.25$

That is the **ratio**, and it says the new price is $125\%$ of the old one. The *change* is the extra $25\%$ on top.

$$\text{ratio} = 1 + \text{percent change as a decimal}$$

So $1.25$ means a $25\%$ increase, and $0.80$ means a $20\%$ decrease. **Answering $125\%$ to "what was the percent increase" reports the whole thing where the question asked for the extra part.**

---

##### Direction Comes From the Sign

**Example 4:** A value falls from \$30 to \$24.

Step 1: The change.
- $24 - 30 = -6$

Step 2: Divide by the original.
- $-6 / 30 = -0.2$

Step 3: Read the sign, then report.
- Negative, so it is a **decrease of** $20\%$.

Subtracting in the other order gives $30 - 24 = 6$ and a positive $20\%$, which is the right size attached to the wrong direction. **Compute new minus original and let the sign tell you which word to use.**

---

##### Percent Changes Do Not Add

**Example 5:** A price rises $10\%$ one year and $20\%$ the next. What is the total percent increase?

Step 1: The second rise is applied to the **already-risen** price, not to the starting one.

Step 2: Multiply the ratios.
- $1.10 \times 1.20 = 1.32$

Step 3: Read the change off the ratio.
- $1.32$ means a $32\%$ increase.

Adding gives $30\%$, and it is short. The missing $2\%$ is the second year's rise applied to the first year's rise. **Percent changes compound; they do not add.**

And a two-period question is not answered by one period. Reporting $20\%$ answers "what happened in year two", which is half the question.

---

##### Decimal to Percent Is One Shift, Not Two

$0.25$ is $25\%$. Multiply by $100$ once.

$2.5\%$ would be $0.025$, which is a tenth of the answer. **If your percent looks ten times too small, you shifted twice.**

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

**Dividing by the new value instead of the original.** It produces a clean percent, it uses only numbers from the stem, and nothing about the answer looks wrong.

The defence is verbal and it takes a second. Before dividing, name the base out loud: "compared with the \$40 it started at." The word "original" is in the formula for a reason, and the stem always tells you which value came first.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Dividing by the new value.** The base is always the starting value.
2. **Reporting the change as the percent.** A rise of $10$ is not $10\%$.
3. **Reporting the ratio as the change.** $1.25$ is $125\%$ of the old, which is a $25\%$ increase.
4. **Adding successive percent changes.** They compound; multiply the ratios.
5. **Shifting the decimal twice.** $0.25$ is $25\%$, not $2.5\%$.

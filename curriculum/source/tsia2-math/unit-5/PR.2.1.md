#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Four Questions, Four Different Answers

Someone asks you to describe a set of test scores with a single number. There is no one right answer, because "typical" can mean several things.

Four words, and each one answers a different question:

| Word | The question it answers | How to get it |
|---|---|---|
| **Mean** | What if everyone shared equally? | add them all, divide by how many |
| **Median** | What is the middle one? | sort, then take the middle |
| **Mode** | Which one happens most? | find the value that repeats most |
| **Range** | How spread out are they? | largest minus smallest |

Three of those describe the **center**. Range is the odd one out: it describes the **spread**, and it is not a measure of center at all.

Here is the thing to internalize before anything else. Every one of these four is easy on its own. The test does not make the arithmetic hard. **It makes you read which one was asked for**, and then puts all four values in the answer choices.

So the first move on every item in this topic is not calculating. It is circling the word.

---

##### Mean

Add every value, then divide by how many values there are.

**Example 1:** Five classmates read $4$, $4$, $7$, $10$, and $5$ books. Find the mean.

Step 1: Add them all.
- $4 + 4 = 8$, $+ 7 = 15$, $+ 10 = 25$, $+ 5 = 30$

Step 2: Count how many values. Five.

Step 3: Divide.
- $30 \div 5 = 6$ books

Step 2 is where points disappear. Count the values, do not assume. With repeats in the list, students often count $4$ distinct values and divide by $4$, getting $7.5$. There are five readers, so the divisor is $5$.

**Zeros count.** If a soccer player scores $0$, $6$, $9$, $7$, $8$ goals across five games, the total is $30$ and the divisor is **five**, giving a mean of $6$. That $0$ is a real game with a real result. Dividing by $4$ because "there was nothing in the first game" gives $7.5$ and is wrong. A zero is data.

---

##### Median

Sort the values, then take the middle one.

**Example 2:** A student earned quiz scores of $12$, $7$, $15$, $9$, and $7$. Find the median.

Step 1: **Sort them.** This is not optional.
- $7$, $7$, $9$, $12$, $15$

Step 2: Find the middle. Five values, so the third one is the middle.
- $9$

Step 3: Check that two values sit on each side. $7$ and $7$ below, $12$ and $15$ above. Balanced.

**The sort is the whole item.** A student who reaches into the unsorted list and grabs its middle entry finds $15$, which is actually the largest value in the set. The list as given tells you nothing about the middle until you order it.

---

##### Median With an Even Count

With an even number of values there are two middles, so **average them**.

**Example 3:** Six students studied $6$, $21$, $10$, $16$, $8$, and $11$ hours. Find the median.

Step 1: Sort.
- $6$, $8$, $10$, $11$, $16$, $21$

Step 2: Six values, so the middles are the third and fourth.
- $10$ and $11$

Step 3: Average them.
- $\frac{10 + 11}{2} = \frac{21}{2} = 10.5$ hours

The median does not have to be a value from the list. $10.5$ is nobody's study time, and it is still the correct median.

Both $10$ and $11$ will be sitting in the answer choices. Picking either one means you stopped at Step 2. **Even count means one more step.**

Counting trick: with $n$ values sorted, the median sits at position $\frac{n+1}{2}$. For $n = 6$ that is $3.5$, meaning halfway between the third and fourth. The formula tells you when you need to average.

---

##### Mode

The value that appears most often.

**Example 4:** A phone received $2$, $2$, $2$, $7$, $9$, $11$, $13$ voicemails over a week. Find the mode.

$2$ appears three times, every other value once. The mode is $\mathbf{2}$.

Two things to keep straight.

**The mode is the value, not the count.** $2$ appeared three times, so the mode is $2$, not $3$. Answering $3$ reports how often rather than which.

**A set can have no mode, or several.** If nothing repeats, there is no mode. If two values tie for most frequent, both are modes.

---

##### Range

Largest minus smallest. One subtraction.

**Example 5:** Daily highs were $14$, $9$, $21$, $6$, and $18$ degrees. Find the range.

Step 1: Identify the extremes. Largest $21$, smallest $6$.

Step 2: Subtract.
- $21 - 6 = 15$ degrees

Reporting $21$ is the classic error. That is the maximum, not the range. **Range is a distance, so it always requires a subtraction.** If you did not subtract, you did not find a range.

---

##### The Mistake That Costs the Most Points

Read this section twice.

**Negative values in a range.**

**Example 6:** Low temperatures one week were $-4$, $9$, $-1$, $12$, $5$, and $-7$ degrees Celsius. Find the range.

Step 1: Sort to find the extremes honestly.
- $-7$, $-4$, $-1$, $5$, $9$, $12$

Step 2: The largest is $12$. The smallest is $-7$. With negatives, **the most negative number is the smallest**, so $-7$ beats $-4$ for smallest even though $7$ is the bigger digit.

Step 3: Subtract, keeping the sign.
- $12 - (-7) = 12 + 7 = 19$ degrees

Step 3 is the point of the whole example. Subtracting a negative **adds**. The range is $19$, not $5$.

Test it against the thermometer. From $-7$ up to $0$ is $7$ degrees of climbing. From $0$ up to $12$ is another $12$. Total climb: $19$. That is the spread, and the thermometer picture will save you every time.

The two wrong paths. Treating $-7$ as $7$ and computing $12 - 7 = 5$ throws away the minus sign. Picking $-4$ as the smallest gives $12 - (-4) = 16$, using the wrong extreme.

---

##### Comparing Mean and Median

Some items ask for the gap between two measures, which means computing both.

**Example 7:** Donations were $\$3$, $\$5$, $\$6$, $\$8$, and $\$28$. By how much does the mean exceed the median?

Step 1: Mean. Add and divide.
- $3 + 5 + 6 + 8 + 28 = 50$, and $50 \div 5 = 10$

Step 2: Median. The list is already sorted, and the middle of five is the third.
- $6$

Step 3: Subtract in the order asked. The mean exceeds the median by:
- $10 - 6 = 4$

The mean is much higher than the median here, and that is not an accident. **One large value drags the mean upward and leaves the median almost untouched.** That $\$28$ is far from the others, and the mean has to absorb it while the median only cares about position.

---

##### Which Measure Survives an Outlier

That last observation is a favourite test question.

Take $10$, $12$, $13$, $14$, $15$ and change the $15$ to $95$.

- **Mean** before: $\frac{64}{5} = 12.8$. After: $\frac{144}{5} = 28.8$. It moved by $16$.
- **Median** before: $13$. After: still $13$. It did not move at all.
- **Range** before: $5$. After: $85$. It moved enormously.

The median is **resistant** to outliers, because it depends on the *position* of the middle value rather than the *size* of the values. The sorted list is still $10$, $12$, $13$, $14$, $95$, and the third entry is still $13$.

The mean uses every value's actual size, so one extreme value pulls it. The range is built entirely from the two extremes, so an extreme value is the worst possible thing to happen to it.

**The median is the most outlier-resistant measure of center.** When a question asks which measure a house price or a salary is best described by, this is why the answer is usually the median.

---

##### Working Backward From a Mean

If you know a mean and a count, you know the **total**. That single idea unlocks every reverse problem.

$$\text{total} = \text{mean} \times \text{count}$$

**Example 8:** A dataset of $3$ values has a mean of $8$. A fourth value, $12$, is added. Find the new mean.

Step 1: Recover the original total.
- $8 \times 3 = 24$

Step 2: Add the new value to the total.
- $24 + 12 = 36$

Step 3: Divide by the **new** count, which is $4$.
- $36 \div 4 = 9$

Two errors to avoid. Averaging the old mean with the new value, $\frac{8 + 12}{2} = 10$, is wrong because the old mean stands for three values and the $12$ for one, so they do not carry equal weight. And dividing the new total by the old count, $36 \div 3 = 12$, forgets that the dataset grew.

**Rebuild from the total. Never average two means.**

**Example 9:** A set of $8$ numbers has a mean of $50$. One number is removed and the remaining $7$ have a mean of $52$. What was removed?

Step 1: Original total.
- $50 \times 8 = 400$

Step 2: New total.
- $52 \times 7 = 364$

Step 3: The removed number is the difference.
- $400 - 364 = 36$

Step 4: Sanity check the direction. Removing a number **raised** the mean, so the number removed must have been **below** the old mean of $50$. And $36 < 50$. Consistent.

Step 4 is worth the ten seconds. If your answer had come out above $50$, you would know immediately that something was backward.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Answering the wrong measure.** All four values sit in the choices. Circle the word mean, median, mode or range before you compute.
2. **Finding a median without sorting.** Sort first, every single time.
3. **Taking one of two middles.** An even count needs the two middles averaged.
4. **Reporting a maximum as a range.** Range is a subtraction. $21$ is not a range.
5. **Averaging two means.** Rebuild from totals instead. A mean of three values does not weigh the same as one new value.

When you miss one below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.

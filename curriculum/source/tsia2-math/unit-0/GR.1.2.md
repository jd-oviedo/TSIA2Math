#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### Two Numbers Run This Entire Topic

Ninety and one hundred eighty.

That is it. Every question in this topic is asking you to subtract a given angle from one of those two numbers, and the only real difficulty is knowing which one. Get that right and the arithmetic is a single subtraction you can do in your head.

So the skill here is not computation. It is **reading the relationship word** in the problem and knowing what number it points at.

---

##### The Size Names

Before the relationships, the vocabulary for a single angle.

| Name | Measure | Everyday example |
|---|---|---|
| acute | less than $90$ degrees | a slice of pizza |
| right | exactly $90$ degrees | the corner of a piece of paper |
| obtuse | between $90$ and $180$ degrees | a reclining chair back |
| straight | exactly $180$ degrees | a flat line |

A full turn all the way around a point is $360$ degrees. That number matters later, and it is also the source of a specific wrong answer, so keep it separate in your head from the two that actually run the topic.

---

##### Complementary: Adds to 90

**Two angles are complementary when their measures add to $90$ degrees.**

If one is $35$, the other is $90 - 35 = 55$.

**Supplementary: adds to 180.**

If one is $110$, the other is $180 - 110 = 70$.

These two get swapped constantly, and swapping them is the single most expensive error in the topic. So pick a memory hook and commit to it.

**The hook that works best:** **C** comes before **S** in the alphabet, and $90$ comes before $180$ on the number line. Complementary is the smaller one.

**A second hook, if you prefer pictures:** **C** for **C**orner. A corner is a right angle, $90$ degrees. **S** for **S**traight. A straight line is $180$ degrees.

Use whichever sticks. Just have one, because under time pressure "which was which" is exactly the thing that evaporates.

---

##### Vertical Angles: Equal, Not Added

Here is where the topic changes shape, and students who are cruising suddenly lose points.

When two straight lines cross, they make four angles. The two angles **directly opposite each other** are called **vertical angles**, and:

$$\text{vertical angles are } \textbf{equal}$$

Not supplementary. Not complementary. **Equal.**

If one angle at an intersection is $130$ degrees, the angle opposite it is also $130$ degrees.

This breaks the pattern of the whole rest of the topic, which is why it gets missed. Every other relationship here is a **sum** rule. Vertical angles are an **equality** rule. There is no subtraction to do at all.

A student who answers $50$ for that $130$ has applied a sum rule to a pair defined by equality. The word "vertical" is the flag: when you see it, stop subtracting.

**Note the name is misleading.** Vertical angles have nothing to do with up and down. Two opposite angles can sit side by side horizontally and still be vertical angles. The name comes from sharing a **vertex**, the point where the lines cross.

---

##### Adjacent Angles on a Line: Add to 180

The other pair at that same intersection.

Two angles that sit **next to each other** and together form a straight line are **adjacent** and **supplementary**. Their measures add to $180$.

So one intersection gives you both rules at once.

**Example 1:** Two lines cross. One of the four angles measures $130$ degrees. Find all four.

Step 1: The angle **opposite** the $130$ is vertical to it, so it is also $130$.

Step 2: An angle **next to** the $130$ forms a straight line with it, so it is $180 - 130 = 50$.

Step 3: The angle opposite that $50$ is vertical to it, so it is $50$ too.

The four angles are $130$, $50$, $130$, $50$.

Step 4: Check the total. $130 + 50 + 130 + 50 = 360$, a full turn around the point. Correct.

**That check is worth keeping.** The four angles at any intersection always total $360$. If yours do not, one of the two rules got applied in the wrong place.

But notice: the total being $360$ does not mean you subtract from $360$. Finding one angle from another uses $180$ (adjacent) or equality (vertical). The $360$ is a check on all four together, and reaching for it to find a single angle is a distinct, common mistake.

---

##### Working With Variables

The relationship gives you an equation. Write it down before solving.

**Example 2:** Two lines intersect. One angle measures $x$ degrees and the angle adjacent to it measures $3x$ degrees. Find $x$.

Step 1: Adjacent angles on a line are supplementary, so they add to $180$.
- $x + 3x = 180$

Step 2: Solve.
- $4x = 180$, so $x = 45$

Step 3: Answer what was asked. The question asked for $x$, which is $45$. It did **not** ask for the other angle, $3x = 135$.

**Example 3:** Two vertical angles measure $(3x - 5)$ degrees and $(x + 25)$ degrees. Find $x$.

Step 1: Vertical angles are equal. Set the expressions equal, do not add them.
- $3x - 5 = x + 25$

Step 2: Solve.
- $2x = 30$, so $x = 15$

Step 3: Check. $3(15) - 5 = 40$ and $15 + 25 = 40$. Equal, as vertical angles must be.

A student who sets these two expressions to sum to $180$ gets $x = 40$, and the check exposes it instantly: $3(40) - 5 = 115$ and $40 + 25 = 65$, which are not equal, so they were never vertical angles under that reading.

---

##### Chained Relationships

The hardest items give you two relationships in a row.

**Example 4:** Angle A and angle B are complementary. Angle B and angle C are supplementary. If angle A measures $28$ degrees, find angle C.

Step 1: A and B are complementary, so they add to $90$.
- $B = 90 - 28 = 62$

Step 2: B and C are supplementary, so they add to $180$.
- $C = 180 - 62 = 118$

Two traps live in that second step.

**Stopping at B.** Sixty-two is a real number in this problem and it is not the answer. Read the last line again before you circle.

**Skipping B.** Jumping straight to $180 - 28 = 152$ pretends A and C are supplementary, which the problem never said. The chain has two links and you have to walk both.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Swapping complementary and supplementary.** C before S, $90$ before $180$. Or C for Corner, S for Straight.
2. **Subtracting for vertical angles.** They are equal. There is nothing to subtract.
3. **Reaching for $360$.** The four angles at an intersection total $360$, but finding one angle from another uses $180$ or equality.
4. **Answering the middle step.** In a chained problem, the first angle you find is usually not the one asked for.

When you miss one below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.

#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### The Marks Are Not Always Worth One

Here is the assumption that costs students this entire topic: that each little line on a scale is worth $1$.

Sometimes it is. Usually it is not. On a ruler each small line is $0.1$ cm. On a bathroom scale it might be $2$ pounds. On a pressure gauge it might be $2.5$ psi. **The scale tells you what its marks are worth, and you have to work it out before you read anything.**

So every scale question is really two questions in order:

1. **What is one space worth?**
2. **How many spaces past a label is the pointer?**

Answer those two and multiply. That is the whole topic.

---

##### Finding What One Space Is Worth

$$\text{value of one space} = \frac{\text{difference between two labels}}{\text{number of spaces between them}}$$

**Example 1:** A ruler is labelled at $4$ cm and $5$ cm, and there are $10$ equal spaces between those two labels. What is each space worth?

Step 1: The difference between the labels is $5 - 4 = 1$ cm.

Step 2: That $1$ cm is split into $10$ spaces.

Step 3: Divide.
- $1 \div 10 = 0.1$ cm per space

So each small line is a tenth of a centimetre, which is a millimetre.

**Example 2:** A thermometer is labelled every $10$ degrees, with $5$ equal spaces between labels.

- $10 \div 5 = 2$ degrees per space

Two things to notice. The answer is not $1$, and it is not $10$. It is a number you had to compute, and skipping that computation is the single most common failure in this topic.

---

##### Marks Versus Spaces: Where Everyone Loses a Point

This is the subtle one, and it is worth reading twice.

If a problem says there are **$5$ marks between** two labels, that is **not** $5$ spaces.

Picture it. Put the two labels down, then drop $5$ lines in between them. Those $5$ lines cut the gap into **$6$ pieces**.

$$n \text{ marks between two labels create } n + 1 \text{ spaces}$$

**Example 3:** A dial is labelled at $40$ and $70$, with $5$ marks between them dividing the interval into equal spaces. What is one space worth?

Step 1: $5$ marks make $6$ spaces.

Step 2: The difference between labels is $70 - 40 = 30$.

Step 3: Divide by the number of **spaces**, not marks.
- $30 \div 6 = 5$ per space

A student who divides by $5$ gets $6$, and every reading after that is wrong by $20\%$.

**The reliable habit:** count the **gaps**, not the lines. Put your finger in the spaces between marks and count those. Fence posts and fence panels: five posts in a row make four panels, and it is the panels you are measuring.

If instead the problem says "divided into $5$ equal spaces," then it is $5$, and no adjustment is needed. Read which one the problem gave you.

---

##### Reading the Pointer

Once you know what a space is worth, the reading is a starting label plus a count.

$$\text{reading} = \text{lower label} + (\text{spaces past it}) \times (\text{value of one space})$$

**Example 4:** A measuring cup is labelled at $200$ mL and $300$ mL with $4$ equal spaces between them. The liquid sits $3$ spaces above the $200$ mL mark. How much liquid is there?

Step 1: Value of one space.
- $(300 - 200) \div 4 = 25$ mL

Step 2: Three spaces past the $200$ label.
- $3 \times 25 = 75$

Step 3: Add to the starting label.
- $200 + 75 = 275$ mL

Step 4: Sanity check. The reading must land between the two labels it sits between, and $275$ is between $200$ and $300$. It is also three quarters of the way up, which matches three spaces out of four.

That last check is free and it catches almost everything. **A reading must fall between the labels on either side of it.** If yours does not, go back.

---

##### Three Shortcuts That Are Really Errors

**Rounding to the nearest label.** The pointer is at $275$, and the student writes $300$ because $300$ is the number actually printed on the cup. Labels are landmarks, not answers. If the pointer is between two labels, the answer is between two labels.

**Reading only the whole part.** An object's edge sits $4$ small spaces past the $7$ cm mark on a ruler, and the student writes $7$ cm. The tenths were the entire question. When a scale is subdivided, the subdivisions are what you are being tested on.

**Counting spaces from the wrong label.** If the pointer sits between $50$ and $75$, count up from $50$. Counting down from $75$ works too, but you must then subtract, and mixing the two directions is where signs go missing.

---

##### Two Levels of Subdivision

Finer instruments subdivide twice, and the method just nests.

**Example 5:** A kitchen scale is labelled at $0$ g and $500$ g, with $10$ equal major spaces between them. Each major space is divided into $5$ equal fine marks. A package's pointer sits $2$ major spaces plus $3$ fine marks above $0$. What is the mass?

Step 1: Value of a major space.
- $500 \div 10 = 50$ g

Step 2: Value of a fine mark.
- $50 \div 5 = 10$ g

Step 3: Add up the two contributions.
- $2 \times 50 = 100$
- $3 \times 10 = 30$
- $100 + 30 = 130$ g

Step 4: Check. The reading sits between the second major space at $100$ and the third at $150$, which is where two major spaces plus a bit should land.

Stopping after step 3's first line and reporting $100$ is the classic miss here. Both contributions count.

---

##### Working Backward

Some questions hand you the reading and ask about the scale.

**Example 6:** A scale is labelled at $30$ and $45$. A pointer sits exactly $4$ equal spaces above the $30$ label and reads $40$. How many equal spaces lie between the two labels?

Step 1: The pointer rose $40 - 30 = 10$ over $4$ spaces, so each space is worth $10 \div 4 = 2.5$.

Step 2: The full gap between labels is $45 - 30 = 15$.

Step 3: Divide the gap by the value of one space.
- $15 \div 2.5 = 6$ spaces

Step 4: Check. Six spaces at $2.5$ each is $15$, which closes the gap exactly.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Assuming each mark is worth $1$.** Compute the value of a space before reading anything.
2. **Counting marks instead of spaces.** $n$ marks between two labels make $n+1$ spaces. Count the gaps.
3. **Snapping to the nearest printed label.** A pointer between two labels has a value between two labels.
4. **Reading the whole number and dropping the subdivisions.** The subdivisions are the question.

When you miss one below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.

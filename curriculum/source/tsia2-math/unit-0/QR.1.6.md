#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### You Round Every Day and Get It Right

Somebody asks what time it is. It is $2{:}47$. You say "almost three." Somebody asks what a jacket cost. It was \$68.42. You say "about seventy bucks."

You have never once said "it is two point seven eight three hours." You round constantly, correctly, without thinking about it.

So this topic is not about learning to round. It is about doing it **to the exact place somebody names**, under time pressure, without your hand slipping into a neighbouring column. Nearly every point lost here is lost to one of four mechanical slips, and all four are avoidable once you have a routine.

---

##### The Routine

Three steps. Do them in this order every single time.

1. **Find the place you were asked for.** Put your finger on that digit. Literally.
2. **Look at the digit immediately to its right.** That one digit, and no other, is the **reference digit**.
3. **Decide.** If the reference digit is $5$ or more, round up. If it is $4$ or less, round down.

Then rewrite the number: everything to the left of your place stays, your digit changes or does not, and everything to the right becomes zeros or disappears.

**Example 1:** Round $4{,}738$ to the nearest hundred.

Step 1: The hundreds digit is $7$.

Step 2: The digit immediately right of it is $3$, in the tens place.

Step 3: $3$ is less than $5$, so round down. The $7$ stays.

Step 4: Rewrite. Everything right of the hundreds place becomes zeros.
- $4{,}700$

Notice what did **not** happen. The $8$ in the ones place never got a vote. Only the reference digit decides.

---

##### The Rule Sounds Backward, So Say It Out Loud

"Five or more, round up. Four or less, round down."

$5$ rounds **up**. That surprises people because $5$ sits exactly in the middle. It is a convention, and it is the one every standardized test uses. Memorize it as a sentence, not as an idea.

$3.5$ rounds to $4$. Not $3$.

---

##### Decimals Work the Same Way, With One Extra Trap

The place names to the right of the decimal point:

| Place | Position | Example digit in $0.4982$ |
|---|---|---|
| tenths | first after the point | $4$ |
| hundredths | second after the point | $9$ |
| thousandths | third after the point | $8$ |
| ten-thousandths | fourth after the point | $2$ |

**Example 2:** Round $0.0472$ to the nearest hundredth.

Step 1: The hundredths digit is the second one after the point. That is $4$.

Step 2: The reference digit is $7$.

Step 3: $7$ is $5$ or more, so round up. The $4$ becomes $5$.

Step 4: Rewrite, stopping at the hundredths place.
- $0.05$

The extra trap with decimals is **stopping in the wrong column**. A student who reports $0.047$ has rounded to the nearest thousandth. The arithmetic was right; the destination was wrong. That is why step 1 is "put your finger on the digit" and not "eyeball it."

---

##### When the Digit Is a 9: The Carry

This is where most of the real mistakes happen, and it is worth slowing down for.

**Example 3:** Round $4{,}963$ to the nearest hundred.

Step 1: The hundreds digit is $9$.

Step 2: The reference digit is $6$.

Step 3: $6$ is $5$ or more, so round up. But you cannot write a $10$ in a single column.

Step 4: The $9$ becomes $0$ **and the carry moves left**. The $4$ in the thousands place becomes $5$.
- $5{,}000$

Two ways this goes wrong.

**Forgetting to carry.** A student turns the $9$ into a $0$ and writes $4{,}000$. That is $963$ less than the number they started with, when rounding to the nearest hundred should never move a number by more than $50$.

**Writing the $10$ literally.** A student writes $4{,}1000$ or $4{,}10$, keeping every place and jamming a two-digit number into one column.

**The check that catches both:** a rounded number should always land near the original. Round to the nearest hundred and your answer is within $50$. Round to the nearest tenth and it is within $0.05$. If your answer is nowhere near where you started, the carry went wrong.

The same thing happens with decimals. $0.4982$ to the nearest hundredth: the hundredths digit is $9$, the reference digit is $8$, so round up. The $9$ rolls to $0$ and carries into the tenths, giving $0.50$.

---

##### Round Once, From the Original

Here is a trap that catches careful students specifically, because it comes from being thorough.

**Example 4:** Round $4.45$ to the nearest whole number.

The tempting method is to work in stages: round $4.45$ to the nearest tenth, which gives $4.5$, then round $4.5$ to the nearest whole number, which gives $5$.

That is wrong. The answer is $4$.

Do it in one step from the original number. The whole-number place holds $4$. The reference digit is the tenths digit, which is $4$. Four or less, round down. The answer is $4$.

The staged version rounds the number twice, and the first rounding pushed $4.45$ up to $4.5$, which then pushed it up again. **Rounding is not something you can do in instalments.** Always go back to the original number and look at the one reference digit.

The same logic says: when a problem asks you to compute and then round, **compute first with the full values, then round once at the end.** A contractor who rounds $12.6$ feet to $13$ and $9.5$ feet to $9$ before multiplying gets $117$ square feet. The honest calculation is $12.6 \times 9.5 = 119.7$, which rounds to $120$. Rounding early cost three square feet of flooring.

---

##### Reading the Place Names in Big Numbers

Large numbers get missed because the place names blur together. Anchor yourself on the commas.

$$148{,}732$$

- $1$ is hundred-thousands
- $4$ is ten-thousands
- $8$ is thousands
- $7$ is hundreds
- $3$ is tens
- $2$ is ones

Round to the nearest ten thousand: the digit is $4$, the reference digit is $8$, so round up to $150{,}000$.

Round to the nearest thousand: the digit is $8$, the reference digit is $7$, so round up to $149{,}000$.

Same number, two different questions, two different answers. Read which place the problem named before you do anything else.

---

##### The Four Traps

1. **Using the wrong reference digit.** Only the digit immediately to the right decides. Not the last digit, not the biggest digit.
2. **Rounding down on a $5$.** Five or more rounds up. Always.
3. **Stopping in the wrong place.** The nearest hundredth is not the nearest thousandth. Put your finger on the digit first.
4. **Rounding in stages.** Go back to the original number every time. And when a problem asks you to compute, compute first and round once at the end.

When you miss one below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.

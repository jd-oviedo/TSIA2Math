#### **Part 1: Guided Notes**

##### The Bank Account Test

You have \$40 in an account. You spend \$65. You already know you are \$25 in the hole, and you knew it without writing anything down.

That is $40 - 65 = -25$. You did signed arithmetic in your head, correctly, before anyone taught you a rule for it.

This topic is the whole toolkit for arithmetic on **rational numbers**, which is a formal name for a very ordinary collection: whole numbers, negatives, fractions, and decimals. Anything you can write as one integer over another. Almost every number on the TSIA2 is one of these, so almost every question is built on the four operations you will practice here.

The good news is that you already have the instincts. What you need is a written-down rule for each case, so you are not guessing under time pressure.

---

##### Signed Numbers: Two Rules, Not Twelve

Students try to memorize a table of sign rules and it collapses under them. There are only two ideas.

**Idea 1: Adding and subtracting is about direction on a number line.**

Think of positive as moving right and negative as moving left.

- $-8 + 5$. Start at $-8$, move $5$ to the right. You land on $-3$.
- $-8 - 5$. Start at $-8$, move $5$ further left. You land on $-13$.

When the two signs are **different**, you are moving back toward zero, so **subtract the magnitudes** and keep the sign of whichever number was farther from zero.

- $-8 + 5$: magnitudes $8$ and $5$, difference $3$. The $8$ was farther from zero and it was negative, so the answer is $-3$.

When the two signs are the **same**, you keep going the same direction, so **add the magnitudes** and keep that shared sign.

- $-8 - 5 = -13$. Both moves went left.

**Idea 2: Subtracting a negative is adding.**

$$5 - (-3) = 5 + 3 = 8$$

Taking away a debt makes you richer. Two minus signs in a row cancel into a plus. This is the single most missed rule in the topic, so say it out loud when you see it: "minus a negative is plus."

**For multiplication and division, count the minus signs.**

- An **even** number of negatives gives a **positive** answer.
- An **odd** number of negatives gives a **negative** answer.

$$-6 \times (-4) = 24 \qquad -6 \times 4 = -24 \qquad -24 \div (-4) = 6$$

Notice this rule is completely different from the addition rule. $-6 + (-4) = -10$, but $-6 \times (-4) = 24$. Same two numbers, same two signs, opposite outcome. Check which operation you are actually doing before you touch the signs.

##### Fractions: Multiply Is Easy, Add Is the Work

Here is the thing nobody says clearly enough: **multiplying fractions is easier than adding them.** Most students expect the opposite and get ambushed.

**To multiply, go straight across.**

$$\frac{3}{5} \times \frac{2}{7} = \frac{3 \times 2}{5 \times 7} = \frac{6}{35}$$

No common denominator. No conversion. Top times top, bottom times bottom.

**To divide, flip the second fraction and multiply.**

$$\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{2}{3} = \frac{3}{4} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{9}{8}$$

The flipped fraction is called the **reciprocal**. Two things to be careful about: flip the **second** one, not the first, and flip it **before** you multiply, not after.

**To add or subtract, you need a common denominator first.**

You cannot add fifths to eighths any more than you can add feet to gallons. The denominators have to match before the numerators can combine.

**Example 1:** $\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{4}$

Step 1: Find a common denominator. The smallest number both $6$ and $4$ divide into is $12$.

Step 2: Rewrite each fraction with denominator $12$. Whatever you multiply the bottom by, multiply the top by the same thing.
- $\frac{5}{6} = \frac{5 \times 2}{6 \times 2} = \frac{10}{12}$
- $\frac{1}{4} = \frac{1 \times 3}{4 \times 3} = \frac{3}{12}$

Step 3: Now subtract the numerators and keep the denominator.
- $\frac{10}{12} - \frac{3}{12} = \frac{7}{12}$

**The denominators never combine.** Not when adding, not when subtracting. A student who writes $\frac{5}{6} - \frac{1}{4} = \frac{4}{2}$ has subtracted the bottoms, which turns a number just under $1$ into the number $2$. Sanity check caught it instantly.

##### Decimals: Line Up, or Count Up

The two rules look similar and they are not.

**Adding and subtracting: line up the decimal points.** Pad with zeros so both numbers have the same number of places.

$$0.70 + 0.25 = 0.95$$

Writing $0.7$ as $0.70$ costs nothing and prevents the classic error, which is shoving the digits right and adding $7 + 25 = 32$ to get $0.32$.

**Multiplying: ignore the points, then count them.** Multiply as though the decimals were whole numbers, then give the answer as many decimal places as the two factors had **combined**.

**Example 2:** $0.4 \times 0.3$

Step 1: Multiply as whole numbers. $4 \times 3 = 12$.

Step 2: Count decimal places in the factors. $0.4$ has one, $0.3$ has one. That is two total.

Step 3: Put two decimal places in the answer. $0.12$.

Check it for sense: you took a bit less than half of a bit less than a third. The answer should be small. $0.12$ is small. $1.2$ would be bigger than either factor, which multiplying by a number under $1$ can never do.

That sanity check is worth more than the rule. **Multiplying by a number less than $1$ always makes things smaller.** If your product came out bigger than what you started with, you miscounted the places.

---

##### Mixed Numbers: Convert First

A mixed number like $2\frac{1}{2}$ is really $2 + \frac{1}{2}$, and that hidden plus sign causes trouble. Before multiplying or dividing, turn it into a single fraction.

$$2\frac{1}{2} = \frac{2 \times 2 + 1}{2} = \frac{5}{2}$$

**Example 3:** A recipe calls for $2\frac{1}{2}$ cups of flour, and you are making $\frac{3}{4}$ of the recipe.

Step 1: Convert. $2\frac{1}{2} = \frac{5}{2}$.

Step 2: "Of" means multiply.
- $\frac{5}{2} \times \frac{3}{4} = \frac{15}{8}$

Step 3: Convert back to a mixed number. $15 \div 8 = 1$ remainder $7$.
- $\frac{15}{8} = 1\frac{7}{8}$ cups

Step 4: Sanity check. Three quarters of two and a half should be under two and a half but not tiny. $1\frac{7}{8}$ sits right.

The trap here is multiplying only the $2$ and forgetting the $\frac{1}{2}$, which gives $1\frac{1}{2}$. The whole number and the fraction are one quantity. Both parts come along.

##### Operations in Context

Word problems hide the signs in the verbs. Translate before you compute.

**Example 4:** At $6$ a.m. the temperature was $-4$ degrees Fahrenheit. By noon it had risen $11$ degrees. By midnight it had fallen $9$ degrees. What was the midnight temperature?

Step 1: Write the signed expression. "Risen" is a plus, "fallen" is a minus.
- $-4 + 11 - 9$

Step 2: Work left to right.
- $-4 + 11 = 7$
- $7 - 9 = -2$

So the midnight temperature was $-2$ degrees Fahrenheit.

Two failures live here. One is treating "risen" as a fall, which gives $-4 - 11 - 9 = -24$. The other is stopping at noon and reporting $7$, which is a real number in the problem but not the one that was asked for. Read the last sentence of the question again before you circle anything.

---

##### The Five Traps

1. **Mixing up the addition sign rule with the multiplication sign rule.** $-6 + (-4) = -10$ but $-6 \times (-4) = 24$. Check the operation first.
2. **Forgetting that minus a negative is plus.** $5 - (-3) = 8$, not $2$.
3. **Combining denominators.** Adding or subtracting fractions never touches the bottoms once they match.
4. **Right-aligning decimals instead of lining up the point.** Pad with zeros: $0.70 + 0.25$, not $0.7 + 0.25$ shoved right.
5. **Answering the intermediate step.** The number you computed halfway through is not the answer unless the question asked for it.

When you miss one below, name the trap. Naming it is how you stop repeating it.
